# 401(k) Loans Jumped 80%. Here's What Borrowing From Your Retirement Actually Costs

> USA Today reported this week that 401(k) loans jumped 80% as more workers tap their retirement savings early. Borrowing from yourself sounds harmless — the interest goes back into your own account, after all. But a 401(k) loan has real costs, and one trap that can turn a short-term fix into a tax bill. Here's how it works, when it makes sense, and what to try first.

Reviewed: November 14, 2026

## How a 401(k) loan works

A 401(k) loan is not a withdrawal. You borrow from your own vested balance and pay yourself back, usually through automatic payroll deductions over five years or less. If the loan is for buying your primary home, your plan may allow a longer repayment period.

The IRS sets the borrowing ceiling: in most plans you can borrow up to 50% of your vested balance, or $50,000, whichever is less. Your plan's own rules may be stricter — some cap loans lower, limit you to one outstanding loan at a time, or charge a small origination fee. And not every plan offers loans at all; check with your plan administrator before counting on one.

There is no credit check and the loan is not reported to the credit bureaus, which is part of the appeal. But "no credit check" does not mean "no cost."

## The three costs nobody mentions

**The money stops growing.** While your loan balance is out of the account, it is not invested. If the market rises during those years, you miss that growth — and the growth on that growth. A $10,000 loan repaid over five years is not just $10,000 out of the market; it is five years of compounding you never get back.

**You pay interest with after-tax dollars — then pay tax on it again.** The interest on a 401(k) loan goes back into your account, which sounds like a wash. It is not. You repay the interest with money that has already been taxed, and when you eventually withdraw it in retirement, it is taxed a second time as ordinary income. You are the only borrower who pays tax twice on the same interest.

**Some plans suspend your contributions.** A few plans stop or reduce your contributions while a loan is outstanding, which can also mean losing part of your employer match. That is free money walking out the door on top of everything else.

## The job-loss trap

This is the one that hurts people. If you leave your job — laid off, fired, or quit — the outstanding loan balance typically becomes due by your tax return due date (including extensions) for the year you separated. Miss that deadline and the unpaid balance is treated as a distribution: it counts as taxable income, and if you are under 59½, the 10% early-withdrawal penalty applies on top.

That is how a $8,000 loan becomes a surprise tax bill in the same year you lost your income. If your job feels at all unstable, or you are thinking about changing jobs, that risk alone should make you pause.

## When a 401(k) loan can make sense

It is not always a bad move. Compared with a credit card charging 20%+ interest, a 401(k) loan is usually cheaper, and the interest at least lands in your own account. It can be the least-bad option for a genuine emergency — a medical bill, a car repair that keeps you employed — when the alternatives are high-interest debt or falling behind on rent.

The honest test: can you repay it on schedule even if your income drops, and is this a one-time emergency rather than a symptom of a budget that does not work? If the answer to either is no, the loan is papering over a bigger problem.

## What to try first

- **Your emergency fund.** This is exactly what it is for. If you do not have one, a small one — even $1,000 — is worth building before you borrow from retirement.
- **A hardship withdrawal is not a better option.** It is permanent: the money leaves the account for good, it is taxed, and the 10% penalty usually still applies. A loan at least gets repaid.
- **Negotiate the bill.** Hospitals, utilities, and many creditors have hardship programs or payment plans that cost far less than raiding retirement. Ask before you borrow.
- **A personal loan from a credit union.** Rates are usually far below a credit card, and your retirement account stays untouched and compounding.

## The bottom line

A 401(k) loan is the cheapest way to borrow from the worst possible source: your future self. The 80% jump in loans tells us a lot of workers are under real financial pressure — and that makes it more important, not less, to count the full cost before signing. If you do borrow, borrow the smallest amount that solves the problem, repay it as fast as your plan allows, and keep contributing if you can. Your 65-year-old self is the one paying for this decision either way.
