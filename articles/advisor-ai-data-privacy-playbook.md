# Is Your Financial Advisor Using AI — and Your Personal Data — Responsibly?

> Financial advisors are adopting AI fast: tools that summarize your meetings, draft your plan, scan your portfolio, and answer your questions between appointments. Some of it genuinely helps. Some of it quietly sends your financial life to third-party vendors. Your advisor's job is to protect your interests — and that now includes how your data flows through their AI tools. Here's what to ask before you assume it's handled.

Reviewed: November 26, 2026

## Where AI shows up in an advisor's office

You may never see the AI directly. It usually works behind the scenes:

- **Meeting notes and summaries.** A tool records your call or meeting, transcribes it, and drafts a summary and follow-up tasks for your advisor.
- **Portfolio and planning software.** AI features inside the planning and portfolio tools your advisor already uses, flagging accounts to rebalance or modeling scenarios.
- **Client chatbots and email drafts.** A chatbot answers routine questions on the firm's website, or an AI drafts the email your advisor sends you.
- **Document review.** AI scans your tax returns, statements, and estate documents to pull out the numbers your advisor needs.

None of this is inherently bad — a good summary tool means your advisor actually remembers what you said. The question is not whether AI is involved, but what data it touches and where that data goes.

## Why your data deserves extra care

An advisor holds some of the most sensitive information you have: your Social Security number, account numbers and balances, income, debts, tax returns, estate plans, and your goals for your family. That is a richer file than your bank or your employer holds, because it combines the money with the story behind it.

AI tools change the risk in two ways. First, data that used to stay inside the firm's office may now be processed by an outside vendor's servers. Second, some AI products improve by learning from the data they process, so your information could shape a model that serves other firms' clients. A responsible firm has contracts and settings that prevent both — not every firm has checked.

## What the rules actually require

Advisors are fiduciaries: they must act in your best interest and disclose conflicts and material facts. That duty doesn't disappear when a tool does the work — the advisor is still responsible for the recommendation, even if an AI drafted it.

Regulators have noticed. The SEC has brought enforcement cases against advisers who exaggerated their AI capabilities — so-called "AI washing" — and both the SEC and FINRA have warned firms that AI tools must be supervised, tested, and documented like any other part of the business. Advisors also remain bound by privacy rules that limit how they share your nonpublic personal information with outside parties.

In practice, your advisor should be able to explain, in plain language, which tools they use, what data they receive, and what happens to it afterward. If they can't, that is itself an answer.

## Five questions to ask your advisor

You don't need to be technical. Ask these in your next meeting or by email, and keep the answers:

1. **"Which AI tools do you use on my account, and what do they do?"** You want a list, not a shrug. Meeting summarizers, planning software, and chatbots are the common ones.
2. **"Does any of my data leave your firm?"** If a tool is hosted by a third-party vendor, your data goes to that vendor. Ask who the vendors are and whether your advisor has a written agreement with them covering confidentiality and data security.
3. **"Is my data used to train AI models?"** Many business AI products offer a setting that excludes your data from training. A careful firm has it switched on — or uses tools that never train on client data at all.
4. **"Who reviews what the AI produces?"** AI drafts plans, summaries, and recommendations. A human advisor should review anything that affects your money before it reaches you. Ask how that review works.
5. **"Can I opt out?"** If you'd rather your meetings not be recorded or your documents not be processed by a particular tool, ask whether that's an option.

## Red flags worth acting on

- **Vague answers or defensiveness.** "Don't worry, it's all secure" is not an answer. A firm that takes this seriously has specifics ready.
- **AI-generated advice with no human check.** If a recommendation reads like a template, contains errors about your situation, or contradicts what you discussed, push back and ask who reviewed it.
- **Surprise recordings.** If a meeting is being recorded or transcribed, you should be told before it starts, not after.
- **New tools with no notice.** If your advisor switches platforms or adds a client portal with AI features, they should tell you what changed and why.

## What to do if you're not comfortable

Most advisors will welcome the conversation — the careful ones already have answers prepared, because their compliance teams asked the same questions.

If the answers are evasive, or the firm can't explain where your data goes, you have options. Ask for your preferences to be noted in writing. Limit what you share through the firm's portal until you understand the tools. And if the firm's approach to your data doesn't match your comfort level, that is a legitimate reason to look for an advisor whose practices do.

## The bottom line

AI in financial advice is not going away, and much of it is genuinely useful — but only if the data handling is sound. Your advisor works for you, and part of that job is being able to say clearly: here is the tool, here is what it sees, here is where your data goes, and here is the human who checks its work. Ask the questions. A good advisor will have good answers.
