# AI Tools That Read Your Bank Account: Claude Personal Finance vs. ChatGPT Finances

> Anthropic launched Claude Personal Finance on November 5, 2026, and OpenAI has its own version, Finances in ChatGPT. Both let an AI chatbot look at your real bank and card accounts and answer questions about your money. Here's what they actually do, what they can see, and the five checks to run before you link anything.

Reviewed: November 10, 2026

## What just launched

On November 5, 2026, Anthropic opened a Personal Finance section inside Claude. Paying US subscribers can link bank and card accounts and ask Claude questions about their own money — "Where did my spending jump last month?" or "Which subscriptions am I still paying for?"

It's a beta, rolling out gradually, and it arrives weeks after OpenAI's equivalent, Finances in ChatGPT. Two of the biggest AI companies are now competing to be the place you ask money questions.

## What Claude can actually see

You connect through Plaid, the service that links bank accounts to apps like Venmo. You sign in to your bank inside Plaid, so Claude never sees your password, and you pick which accounts to share — up to 16 banks.

For the accounts you share, Claude can read:

- The bank's name, and each account's name, type, and last four digits
- Balances and credit limits
- Up to 24 months of transactions — amount, date, merchant, category, and the description your bank wrote

The dashboard adds widgets for spending, cash flow, accounts, subscriptions, upcoming bills, and short observations from Claude, which you can add, remove, and rearrange.

## What it costs, and who can use it

Personal Finance is included at no extra charge for eligible subscribers, but eligibility is narrow:

- **Pro:** $20 a month, or $17 a month billed annually ($200 up front)
- **Max:** $100 a month
- **Ultra:** $200 a month

Free, Team, and Enterprise accounts don't get it, and it's US-only during the beta. A paid account may not see the feature yet — the rollout is gradual.

ChatGPT's version is the cheaper way in. Finances in ChatGPT is available on the Free, Go, Plus, and Pro tiers, so the lowest price is $0. If you only want to try an AI looking at your accounts, that's the obvious starting point before paying $20 a month for Claude.

## What it's genuinely good for

Used honestly, this is a faster version of work you could already do in a spreadsheet. The real uses are boring:

- **Finding leaks.** Ask it to list every recurring charge in the last 90 days. Forgotten subscriptions are the easiest money these tools find, because the AI reads your actual transactions instead of a budget you wrote from memory.
- **Spotting a change.** "What spending category grew most versus the previous three months?" catches lifestyle creep early.
- **Cash-flow checks.** "How much is coming in and going out this month, and which bills land before payday?" is more useful than any single account balance.
- **Preparing for a human.** Ask the AI to summarize your last six months before you call your bank or sit down with a planner.

None of this requires trusting the AI's judgment. You're asking it to organize and summarize your own data, then checking its answers against the statements.

## What it can't do — and where people will get hurt

The danger isn't that the AI moves your money. It's treating a summary as advice:

- **It can't move money.** Access is read-only: Claude can't move money, pay a bill, cancel a subscription, or make an investment. It also doesn't read your investment holdings, and it isn't designed to give investment advice.
- **It doesn't know your goals.** An AI that sees your transactions doesn't know you're saving for a house deposit, or that the "wasteful" spending was a one-off medical bill. Its suggestions are generic by design.
- **It can be confidently wrong.** Transaction data is messy — a refund can look like income, a transfer between your own accounts can look like spending. Verify a surprising answer against the bank statement before acting on it.
- **It isn't a fiduciary.** Neither tool owes you the duty of care a licensed advisor does. The footer on this site says the same thing about our guides: this is educational content, not financial advice. That applies double to a chatbot.
- **Linking accounts is a privacy decision, not a free trial.** You're giving a third party read access to up to two years of your financial life. That's a bigger deal than trying a new app — read the data-use terms first, not after.

## Five checks before you link an account

1. **Confirm read-only in the product's own help pages.** The documentation should say access is read-only and that it can't move money.
2. **Link the fewest accounts that answer your question.** If you only want subscription tracking, link one card, not every account you own. You can add more later.
3. **Know how to revoke access.** Before linking, find the disconnect button and the Plaid connection manager. If you can't find it in thirty seconds, don't link yet.
4. **Read the data-use terms.** Check whether your account chats are used for training and whether the tool remembers things across conversations — policies differ between providers and change over time.
5. **Don't paste sensitive information into the chat.** Account numbers, passwords, Social Security numbers, and one-time codes don't belong in a chatbot conversation — the tool already has the data it needs from the link.

## The bottom line

Both tools are genuinely useful as read-only lenses on your own spending — and genuinely limited as anything more. The best use is narrow: link one or two accounts, ask it to find subscriptions and spending changes, verify everything against your statements, and make decisions yourself. If you want a second opinion on investments or a plan for retirement, that's still a job for a human professional, not a chatbot with your bank feed.
