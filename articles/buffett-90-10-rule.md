# Warren Buffett's 90/10 Rule: The Simplest Retirement Portfolio Ever Written Down

> Yahoo Finance asked this week whether Warren Buffett's famous 90/10 rule still holds up for retirement. The rule fits on one line — and that's exactly the point. Here's what it actually says, who it works for, and where it can hurt you.

Reviewed: November 13, 2026

## What the rule actually is

In his 2013 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Buffett published the instructions he left for the trustee managing his wife's inheritance. His advice, in his own words: "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. (I suggest Vanguard's.)"

That's the whole rule. Ninety percent in a low-cost fund that owns the 500 largest U.S. companies, ten percent in short-term government bonds — which behave a lot like cash.

Buffett has repeated the core idea for decades: most investors would do better owning a low-cost index fund than paying a professional to beat the market. He put real money behind that belief with a famous million-dollar bet that an S&P 500 index fund would beat a hand-picked basket of hedge funds over ten years. The index fund gained roughly 126% over the period; the hedge funds averaged about 36%.

## Why something this simple works

Three forces do the heavy lifting:

- **Costs stay near zero.** A very low-cost S&P 500 index fund charges a tiny fraction of what most actively managed funds charge, and that gap compounds for decades. Buffett has said plainly that costs really matter in investing — every dollar paid in fees is a dollar that stops compounding for you.
- **You own the whole market, not a guess.** The S&P 500 is hundreds of profitable companies across every sector. When one stumbles, others carry the weight. You're betting on the U.S. economy over the long run, not on any single company or manager.
- **The cash buffer does a real job.** The 10% in short-term government bonds isn't there for returns — it's there so you never have to sell stocks during a crash to pay the bills. A retiree with a year or two of spending parked in something stable can wait out a downturn instead of locking in losses.

Research by finance professor Javier Estrada adds some reassurance: across 86 different 30-year periods between 1900 and 2014, a 90/10 portfolio withdrawing 4% a year failed only about 2.3% of the time — barely worse than the traditional, far more conservative 60/40 stocks-and-bonds mix, and it ended with more money on average.

## The catch everyone skips: it wasn't written for you

Here's the part that gets lost every time the rule goes viral. Buffett wrote those instructions for a trust with a very long horizon and **no fixed withdrawal schedule**. His wife's trustee doesn't need to pull a set amount out every month to cover groceries. You probably do — and that changes the math.

A 90% stock portfolio can fall 30% or more in a bad bear market. If you're still working and not touching the money, that's a paper loss you can ride out. If you're in year one of retirement, withdrawing 4% a year while the portfolio drops 30%, the combination of falling prices and steady withdrawals permanently shrinks the capital base. That's sequence-of-returns risk, and it's the one thing the rule doesn't protect against.

Two other honest caveats:

- **It's U.S.-only.** An S&P 500 fund holds no foreign companies. Some investors prefer adding international diversification; Buffett's rule doesn't.
- **It assumes you can stay calm.** The rule works only if you actually hold through crashes. If a 30% drop would make you sell everything and sit in cash for years, a gentler mix — say 60/40 — will serve you better in practice, even if it looks worse on paper.

## How to use it in real life

If the philosophy appeals to you, here's the practical version:

1. **Build the emergency fund first.** Three to six months of expenses in a high-yield savings account, before you invest a dollar. The 10% bond slice is a buffer, not an emergency fund.
2. **Use your tax-advantaged accounts.** A 401(k) or IRA holding a low-cost S&P 500 index fund is the natural home for the 90% — and if your 401(k) doesn't offer a cheap S&P 500 option, a broad total-U.S.-market index fund is the same idea.
3. **Keep the 10% boring on purpose.** Short-term Treasury bonds, a Treasury money market fund, or a short-term government bond fund all do the job. The point is stability and instant access, not yield.
4. **Split the portfolio by time horizon, not by age alone.** Money you won't touch for 10-plus years can live close to the 90/10 spirit. Money you'll need in the next three to five years — near-term retirement spending — belongs in something steadier, no matter what your age is.
5. **Rebalance once a year.** If stocks had a great year and you're now at 94/6, sell a little stock and top up the bonds back to 10%. Ten minutes, once a year, and you're done.
6. **Do nothing in a crash.** The entire strategy rests on not selling stocks when they're down. If you can't commit to that, choose a calmer allocation from the start.

## The one-line version

Buffett's 90/10 rule is the best argument ever written for keeping investing simple: 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund, 10% in short-term government bonds, rebalance yearly, and let compounding do the work. Use it for the money with a long horizon, keep near-term spending money safe, and never let a market drop force you to sell. Simple doesn't mean easy — but it does mean you can stop paying for complexity you don't need.
