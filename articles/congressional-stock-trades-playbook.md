# Congress Trades Stocks. Here's How to Read the Disclosures — and Why Copying Them Is a Bad Idea

> Members of Congress have to publicly disclose their stock trades — and lately, the public is paying close attention. Nvidia, the chipmaker at the center of the AI boom, has become one of the most-watched names in those filings. The disclosures are a genuine transparency tool. They are not a stock-picking service, and treating them like one is an expensive mistake. Here's what the filings actually say, where to read them, and how to use them without fooling yourself.

Reviewed: November 25, 2026

## The rule in plain English

The STOCK Act, signed in 2012, says members of Congress and their staff are not exempt from insider trading laws — and it forces their trades into the open. The rules are simple:

- **Any trade over $1,000 counts.** A purchase, sale, or exchange of a stock, bond, or option above $1,000 has to be reported. Trades by a spouse or dependent child count too.
- **The deadline is 45 days, at most.** A member must file within 30 days of learning about a trade, and no later than 45 days after the trade itself — whichever comes first. In practice, most filings land near the deadline.
- **Amounts are reported as ranges, not exact dollars.** A filing says something like "$1,001 to $15,000" or "$1 million to $5 million." You never see the precise amount.
- **Late filings cost $200.** That is the statutory penalty per late report, and it can be waived. Unsurprisingly, late filings are common.

The disclosures cover the trade — the stock, the date, whether it was a buy or a sell, and the size range. They do not cover why the trade happened, and they never show a member's full portfolio.

## Where to read them yourself

Both filings systems are free and public. House disclosures live on the Clerk of the House financial disclosure site, searchable by member name and year. Senate disclosures are on the Secretary of the Senate's electronic financial disclosure system. A handful of free tracker sites republish the filings in a friendlier format, grouped by member and stock, if you'd rather not dig through government PDFs.

That is the entire data source. Every headline about a member buying or selling a stock traces back to one of these filings.

## Why Nvidia keeps showing up

Big technology stocks dominate these disclosures, and Nvidia has become the headline name — a recent round of public discussion focused specifically on who in Washington owns and trades Nvidia stock. But a filing that says a member bought Nvidia tells you only that, in a broad range, weeks after the fact. It does not tell you whether the trade was a long-term holding, a rebalance, or a move by an adviser in a managed account.

## What the disclosures can't tell you

- **They are old news by design.** A trade can be a month and a half old before it becomes public. In a fast-moving stock, the price can move a lot in 45 days.
- **You don't know the size.** The difference between a $1,001 trade and a $15,000 trade is a rounding error in a member's finances but a very different signal — and the filing doesn't tell you which it was.
- **You don't know the motive.** A sale can mean "I think this stock is overvalued" or "I needed cash for a tax bill" or "my adviser rebalanced." The filing looks identical either way.
- **You don't see the whole picture.** The disclosure shows one trade, not the member's total position, cost basis, or what else they own. A "buy" of one stock might sit next to a much larger sale somewhere else.

## Why copying the trades is a bad strategy

It is tempting: if people with access to information are buying, why not follow? Three problems.

**First, you are always late.** By the time a filing is public, the trade is weeks old. If the information edge existed, it was priced in long before you saw it.

**Second, you are copying the range, not the trade.** You can't reconstruct the actual position size, so you can't copy the risk — only the ticker symbol.

**Third, there is no evidence it beats the boring alternative.** The honest benchmark is a broad index fund. Copying disclosed trades, after the lag and with incomplete information, has no track record of outperforming it — and it adds trading costs and concentration risk that an index fund doesn't have.

## How to use the disclosures sensibly

- **Treat them as a transparency tool, not a tip sheet.** Their real value is accountability: voters can see whether a member trades in industries their committee regulates. That's the point of the law.
- **Check your own overlap.** If you hold the same stocks, a filing might prompt you to revisit why you own them. That is a useful nudge, not a reason to buy or sell.
- **Ignore the copy-trading funds and social media accounts built on this data.** They package a 45-day-old, range-sized disclosure as a live signal. The lag is the product's flaw, not a detail.

## The bottom line

Congressional trade disclosures are public, free, and worth knowing about — they exist so voters can watch for conflicts of interest. What they are not is a shortcut to beating the market. A trade that is weeks old, reported in a wide range, with no motive attached, is a record of positioning, not a recommendation. Read them to stay informed. Build your portfolio on your own goals, your time horizon, and a diversified plan — the same way you would if the filings didn't exist at all.
