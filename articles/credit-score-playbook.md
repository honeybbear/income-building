# Your Credit Score, Explained in Plain English — and How to Raise It

> Your credit score is a three-digit number that quietly decides how much you pay to borrow money. Here's what actually moves it, what doesn't, and the fastest honest ways to improve it.

Reviewed: November 5, 2026

## What a credit score actually is

A credit score is a lender's shorthand for one question: how likely are you to pay borrowed money back on time? The most widely used scores, FICO scores, run from 300 to 850. Higher is better, and the score is calculated from the information in your credit reports — the files kept by the three major bureaus: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion.

You don't have one single score. Each bureau keeps its own report, lenders may pull different scoring models, and your score shifts as your report changes. That sounds messy, but it means a small mistake on one report doesn't define you forever — and a good habit repeated for months moves the number in the right direction.

The score matters because it prices your borrowing. A higher score generally means lower interest rates on credit cards, auto loans, and mortgages, and it can affect whether a landlord approves your rental application or whether you need a deposit to open a utility account. The difference between a fair score and a good one can be thousands of dollars over the life of a loan.

## The five things that move your score

FICO publishes the rough weights behind its scores, and they tell you exactly where to focus:

- **Payment history — about 35%.** Paying every bill on time is the single biggest factor. One late payment, especially 30 or more days late, can drag a score down for years, though its impact fades over time.
- **Amounts owed — about 30%.** This is mostly about credit utilization: how much of your available credit you're using. Using a large share of your limits signals risk, even if you pay in full.
- **Length of credit history — about 15%.** Older accounts help. This is why closing your oldest card can hurt: it shortens your average account age.
- **New credit — about 10%.** Each application for new credit typically triggers a hard inquiry, which can shave a few points off temporarily. Several applications in a short window look riskier than one.
- **Credit mix — about 10%.** Having more than one type of credit — say, a credit card and an installment loan — can help slightly, but never take out a loan just to improve your mix.

## The fastest honest ways to raise it

There are no overnight fixes, but these moves work in roughly the order that matters most:

**1. Pay everything on time, starting now.** Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment on every card and loan. A missed payment is the most expensive mistake on this list, and autopay removes the forgetfulness risk entirely. If money is tight, call the lender before the due date — many will work with you, and a hardship arrangement beats a late mark.

**2. Pay down your balances, starting with the highest-utilization card.** A widely used guideline is to keep each card's utilization under 30% of its limit, and lower is better. If a card has a $1,000 limit and a $900 balance, getting that balance under $300 is usually one of the fastest score improvements available. You don't need to pay it to zero — just get it down.

**3. Don't close old cards you no longer use.** If the card has no annual fee, keep it open with an occasional small purchase, paid off in full. Closing it reduces your total available credit (raising your utilization) and eventually shortens your history.

**4. Space out new applications.** If you're planning a mortgage or auto loan, avoid opening new credit cards in the months before you apply. Rate-shopping for a mortgage or auto loan within a short window is generally treated as a single inquiry for scoring purposes, so compare lenders — just do it within a couple of weeks, not spread over months.

**5. Check your reports and dispute errors.** You can get free copies of your credit reports every week from AnnualCreditReport.com, the only federally authorized source. Look for accounts you don't recognize, wrong balances, or payments marked late that you made on time. Disputing a genuine error with the bureau is free, and a corrected error can lift your score. Be wary of any company that charges you to "fix" your credit — the dispute process is something you can do yourself, for free.

## What doesn't move your score

A few common myths are worth clearing up:

- **Checking your own score or report never hurts it.** That's a soft inquiry, not a hard one. Check as often as you like.
- **Your income, savings, and job title aren't in your score.** Lenders may consider income separately when you apply, but it isn't part of the credit score itself.
- **Paying off a loan early isn't always a score win.** It can help your finances, but closing an installment loan can slightly reduce your credit mix. Don't keep a loan open just for the score — pay it off if that's the right financial move.

## How long improvement takes

A score built on missed payments recovers slowly — the late marks stay on your report for up to seven years, but their impact shrinks with each passing year of on-time payments. A score held back mainly by high utilization can improve in as little as one or two billing cycles after you pay balances down, because utilization has no memory: last month's high balance stops mattering once the new, lower balance is reported.

The honest version of "fixing your credit" is unglamorous: pay on time, keep balances low, leave old accounts open, and let time do the rest. There's no shortcut, but there is a reliable path — and it's free.

*This article is educational content, not financial advice. Score ranges and factor weights reflect FICO's published guidance; your actual score depends on the model and report a lender uses.*
