# Delaying Social Security to 70: The Cheapest Annuity You Can Buy

> Retirement researchers keep arriving at the same conclusion: for most people who can afford to wait, delaying Social Security until 70 is the best guaranteed return in retirement planning. Here's the math, the trade-offs, and the three situations where waiting is the wrong call.

Reviewed: November 28, 2026

## What "the cheapest annuity" actually means

An annuity is simple: you hand an insurer a lump sum, and it pays you a monthly check for life. The catch is the price — insurers charge for the guarantee, and the payout is modest. Social Security works the same way, except the government is the insurer, and the price of waiting is just patience.

For every year you delay claiming past your full retirement age, your benefit grows by **8%** — guaranteed, inflation-adjusted, for life. No private annuity on the market offers a guaranteed 8% annual increase plus cost-of-living adjustments. That's why retirement researchers keep calling delay the cheapest annuity you can buy, a framing that made headlines again this week.

## The numbers, on a round example

Your full retirement age is 67 if you were born in 1960 or later. Suppose your benefit at full retirement age would be $1,000 a month. Here's what claiming at each age does to it:

- **Claim at 62:** about $700 a month — a 30% permanent reduction.
- **Claim at 67:** $1,000 a month, your full benefit.
- **Claim at 70:** about $1,240 a month — 24% above your full benefit.

That $1,240 is not a one-time bonus. It is your monthly check for the rest of your life, and it gets every future cost-of-living adjustment on top of the higher base. Over a 20-year retirement, the gap between $700 and $1,240 a month is roughly **$130,000**.

## The break-even question, answered honestly

Waiting only pays off if you live long enough. If you claim at 62 instead of 70, you collect eight extra years of checks, and the higher age-70 check needs time to catch up. The crossover lands roughly around **age 80 or 81** for the 62-versus-70 comparison.

That's the whole decision in one sentence: if you expect to live past your early 80s, waiting wins. The average 65-year-old man lives to about 84 and the average 65-year-old woman to about 87, so for a typical retiree in average health, the odds favor waiting. If your health is poor or your family history points to a shorter life, claiming earlier is the rational choice.

## Three more reasons waiting is powerful

**Your spouse's survivor benefit grows too.** If you're the higher earner in a couple, delaying doesn't just raise your check — it raises the survivor benefit your spouse would receive after you die. For many couples, this is the single most valuable reason to wait, and it's the one most often overlooked.

**There is no point in waiting past 70.** Delayed credits stop at 70. If you haven't claimed by then, you're leaving money on the table for no gain. Age 70 is the ceiling, not a suggestion.

**Working while you wait has a quirk worth knowing.** If you claim before full retirement age and keep earning, the earnings test applies: in 2026, Social Security withholds $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480 if you're under full retirement age all year, and $1 for every $3 above $65,160 in the year you reach full retirement age. Withheld benefits aren't lost — your payment is recalculated upward at full retirement age — but the test is one more reason claiming early while still working full-time rarely makes sense. Once you reach full retirement age, the earnings test disappears entirely.

## When claiming early is the right call

Waiting is the default recommendation, not a universal one. Claim earlier if any of these fit:

1. **You need the money now.** A guaranteed 8% increase is worthless if waiting means draining your savings or going into debt. Social Security exists to be used.
2. **Your health is poor.** If living to 80 looks unlikely, take the checks while you can enjoy them.
3. **You're the lower earner and your spouse is delaying.** Couples often do best with one early claim for cash flow and one delayed claim for the maximum survivor benefit — but run the numbers for your own situation rather than copying a template.

## How to bridge the gap years

The practical obstacle is obvious: waiting until 70 means living without Social Security from 62 to 70. The standard bridge is spending from your own savings first — drawing down a 401(k) or IRA in your 60s so Social Security can grow. That trade is often worth it: you're swapping taxable withdrawals in your 60s for a larger, inflation-protected check in your 70s and beyond. (Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable depending on income, so check your bracket before deciding.)

Before you commit, log in to your my Social Security account at ssa.gov and look at your own estimated benefits at 62, 67, and 70. The generic math above is useful; your numbers are decisive. If the gap between your age-62 and age-70 checks looks small, your earnings history may be the reason — and the decision may matter less than the headlines suggest. If the gap is large, waiting is the cheapest annuity you'll ever buy.

*Sources: Delayed retirement credit of 8% per year and full retirement age of 67 for those born 1960 or later, per the Social Security Administration; 2026 earnings-test limits ($24,480 and $65,160) per SSA's "How Work Affects Your Benefits"; "cheapest annuity" framing and the $700,000 example per 24/7 Wall St., November 27, 2026.*
