# How to Spot a Fake Black Friday Deal

> Not every "deal" saves you money. A recent investigation found that nearly 1 in 3 of Amazon's advertised Black Friday and Cyber Monday deals offered no real savings — and nearly 1 in 10 cost more than the same item weeks earlier. Here's how to check any deal before you buy.

Reviewed: November 17, 2026

## The deal that isn't a deal

Black Friday and Cyber Monday are the biggest shopping days of the year — a record 203 million Americans shopped between Thanksgiving and Cyber Monday in 2025, according to the National Retail Federation. Retailers know you're looking, and the red "SALE" badge does a lot of heavy lifting.

A 2026 investigation by the Groundwork Collaborative, *Deal or Decoy?*, examined more than 350 products Amazon promoted as some of its "best" Black Friday and Cyber Monday deals in 2024 and 2025. The findings were blunt:

- Nearly 1 in 3 of the advertised "deals" cost the same — or more — than the product had sold for in the month before the sale.
- Nearly 1 in 10 were priced *higher* during the sale than in the weeks leading up to it. Among those worse deals, the median sale price was 19% above what shoppers could have paid earlier.
- One example: Hunter rain boots "on sale" for $126.99 on Black Friday 2025 had cost $79.97 nine days earlier. A pair of UGG boots listed at $179.98 on Cyber Monday had been $98.40 about four weeks before.

The trick is simple. A retailer raises the price in the weeks before the sale, then cuts it back to roughly where it started — or not quite — and calls the difference a discount. The strikethrough price looks like a bargain. The price history says otherwise.

## Check the price history before you buy

This is the single most useful habit in holiday shopping, and it takes about thirty seconds.

- **Use a price tracker.** Free tools like CamelCamelCamel (for Amazon) and Keepa show a product's price over the past months. If the "deal" price is the same as the price from three weeks ago, it's not a deal — it's a marketing label.
- **Search the product elsewhere.** Copy the exact model number into a search engine and compare prices at two or three other stores. A genuine sale usually beats the competition; a fake one often doesn't.
- **Be skeptical of the "list price."** A crossed-out "was $200" only means something if the item actually sold at $200 recently. If it sat at $120 all autumn, then $129 "marked down from $200" is a price increase dressed as a discount.

## Four red flags on the deal page

**1. The countdown clock.** "Ends in 2 hours!" creates urgency on purpose. Real deals come back; a countdown is designed to stop you from checking the price history. Close the tab and check first — the deal will usually still be there.

**2. Vague savings claims.** "Save up to 50%!" tells you nothing about the item in your cart. Look at the actual price, not the percentage. A 50% discount on an inflated price is a normal price.

**3. A price that just jumped.** If you have been watching an item and its price rose in the last few weeks, the "sale" may just be returning it to normal. Price trackers make this visible in one glance.

**4. Bundles and "exclusive" versions.** Some sale items are slightly different models made for the event, with different model numbers that price trackers can't match. Compare specs, not just prices — a cheaper model with a worse battery isn't a deal.

## A simple rule for every holiday purchase

Before you buy anything on sale, answer three questions:

- **Do I actually want this?** A discount on something you wouldn't buy at full price is still money out of your pocket. Make a list before the sales start and stick to it.
- **Is this the lowest price in the last 90 days?** If a price tracker says no, wait. Prices cycle constantly, and the same item will often be cheaper again within weeks.
- **Can I pay for it now?** A "deal" bought on a credit card you can't pay off in January isn't a deal — the interest turns the savings into a loss. If it doesn't fit this month's budget, skip it and revisit it after the holidays.

## The one-line version

Ignore the strikethrough price and the countdown clock. Check the price history, compare it at one other store, and only buy it if it's genuinely the cheapest it's been in months — and only if it was already on your list.
