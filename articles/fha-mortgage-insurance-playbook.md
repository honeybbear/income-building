# FHA Mortgage Insurance: Why Some Borrowers Pay It for a Decade — or for Life

> If you have an FHA loan, part of every monthly payment is mortgage insurance — and for many borrowers, that charge never goes away on its own. Here's how FHA mortgage insurance (MIP) actually works, why it can last far longer than the private mortgage insurance on a conventional loan, and the one real way out.

Reviewed: November 21, 2026

## What MIP is — and why FHA loans have it

FHA loans let buyers purchase a home with as little as 3.5% down. The trade-off is mortgage insurance premium, or MIP. It protects the lender — not you — if you stop paying.

Every FHA loan carries two MIP charges:

- **Upfront MIP: 1.75% of the loan amount.** Usually rolled into the loan at closing, which means you also pay interest on it. On a $300,000 loan, that's $5,250.
- **Annual MIP: 0.15% to 0.75% of the loan balance**, split into monthly amounts and added to your payment. Most buyers pay 0.55%. On a $300,000 loan, that's about $1,650 a year — roughly $138 a month — and it drifts down slowly as the balance falls.

## The part that surprises people: how long it lasts

How long you pay annual MIP depends on when your loan was originated and how much you put down. For FHA case numbers assigned on or after June 3, 2013 — which covers nearly every FHA borrower today — the rules are:

- **Less than 10% down:** MIP lasts for the **life of the loan**. There is no equity milestone that cancels it. You can pay the balance down to half the home's value and the premium keeps coming.
**10% or more down:** MIP lasts **11 years**, then cancels automatically.

That is the core difference from a conventional loan. With a conventional loan, private mortgage insurance (PMI) can be cancelled when your balance falls below 80% of the home's original value, and it must be cancelled automatically at 78%. FHA MIP has no such equity trigger — building equity does not end it.

Older loans are different: FHA loans with case numbers assigned before June 3, 2013 can cancel once the balance reaches 78% of the original value (with a five-year minimum payment period for terms longer than 15 years). If your FHA loan is from that era, check your closing documents or ask your servicer for your scheduled cancellation date in writing.

## Why this is in the news

In November 2026, reporting highlighted that some FHA borrowers pay mortgage insurance for a decade or more, and there is an industry push to change the rules — to give FHA borrowers a cancellation path tied to equity, similar to conventional PMI. Nothing has changed yet.

## The one way out: refinance into a conventional loan

If your MIP runs for the life of the loan, the only exit is refinancing out of the FHA loan into a conventional mortgage. That works best when:

- **You have at least 20% equity.** Then the new conventional loan needs no PMI at all — you drop the insurance entirely.
- **The new rate works.** A refinance means a new rate and new closing costs. The monthly savings from dropping MIP have to pay back those costs within the time you plan to stay in the home.

Run the payback math before you sign anything. Take the monthly MIP you're paying now, multiply it by the months you expect to stay, and compare that total against the refinance closing costs plus any rate difference on the remaining balance. If the numbers don't clear that bar, waiting and building more equity is usually the better move.

A refinance also resets your loan clock — a new 30-year term means more years of payments. Weigh that against the monthly savings rather than looking at the payment alone.

## What to do this week if you have an FHA loan

1. **Find your case-number date and down payment.** Both are in your closing documents; your servicer can confirm them. They determine whether your MIP ends after 11 years or never.
2. **Do the lifetime math.** Multiply your monthly MIP by the remaining months of your loan. Seeing the total — often tens of thousands of dollars — makes the refinance decision concrete.
3. **Check your equity.** If home values in your area have risen since you bought, you may be closer to 20% equity than your original down payment suggests. A rough estimate from recent comparable sales is enough to start the conversation.
4. **Price a refinance, don't just wonder about it.** Get a loan estimate from at least two lenders and compare the new rate, closing costs, and the months to break even. A quote costs you an hour; guessing costs you years of premiums.
5. **Don't pay for a "MIP removal" service.** There is no company that can cancel FHA MIP for a fee. The only routes are the automatic cancellation rules above or a refinance.

MIP is the price of a low down payment — and for many borrowers, it's a price that keeps being charged long after the low down payment has stopped mattering. Know which rule applies to your loan, know your equity, and run the refinance math on real numbers.
