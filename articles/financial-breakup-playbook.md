# How to Untangle Your Money After a Breakup

> Splitting up is hard. Splitting the money shouldn't make it harder. Here's a practical checklist for separating your finances — bank accounts, bills, subscriptions, debt, and the awkward Venmo requests — so you can actually move on.

Reviewed: October 23, 2026

Breakups are emotional enough without discovering, three months later, that your ex's streaming charges are still hitting your card or that your joint electric bill is in collections. The good news: almost all of this is administrative. Work through the list below once, in order, and you'll be done.

## Step 1: Freeze the bleeding first

Start with the accounts your ex can still touch:

- **Joint bank accounts.** If you have one, don't just "agree to split it." Go to the bank together (or alone, if your bank's account agreement allows) and close it or remove one person's access. Moving money out without your partner's knowledge can backfire — get any transfer in writing first.
- **Shared credit cards.** Authorized users can keep spending on your card even mid-breakup. Call your issuer and remove your ex as an authorized user today. For a truly joint account (both of you are account holders), you generally can't remove someone unilaterally — you'll need to close the account or have them sign off.
- **Autopays and Venmo/Zelle/payment apps.** Check your linked accounts in every payment app. An ex with your login or a saved card can send money to themselves. Remove their access and change your passwords.

Do this first because everything else can wait a week, but unauthorized spending can't.

## Step 2: Divide shared bills and subscriptions

List every recurring bill you shared — rent, utilities, phone plans, streaming, cloud storage, subscription boxes — and for each one:

1. **Decide who keeps it.** Whoever uses it most usually keeps the account.
2. **Transfer or close.** Transfer ownership through the provider where possible (many allow it with both parties' consent), or close and let the other person open a new one.
3. **Split final balances.** Take a screenshot of each final statement and agree in writing — even just a text message saved — on who owes what. Vague verbal agreements are the number one source of post-breakup Venmo wars.

For the phone plan specifically: family plans often lock both people into a contract. Check the early-termination terms before anyone storms off; sometimes the cheapest option is to keep the shared plan until the contract ends, with clear written terms about who pays.

## Step 3: Handle the debt

Debt is where breakups get expensive. The key principle: **the creditor doesn't care that you broke up.** If your name is on the debt, you're responsible for it in the lender's eyes.

- **Credit cards.** Pay down and close truly joint cards. For authorized-user situations, removing access protects you from new charges, but existing balances stay yours.
- **Personal loans with a co-signer.** If you co-signed, the lender can come after either of you for the full amount. A private agreement between you doesn't bind the lender — put any repayment split in writing, but know the bank isn't part of it.
- **Car loans and leases.** If the car is in both names, one person usually refinances alone or you sell it. Check whether you can even refinance on one income first.

Get a copy of all three credit reports (you're entitled to free weekly reports at AnnualCreditReport.com) and look for any account you don't recognize or forgot about. Dispute anything that's truly not yours.

## Step 4: Protect your identity and paperwork

After a separation, update the unglamorous stuff:

- Change passwords and security questions your ex might know.
- Update your beneficiaries on retirement accounts and life insurance — these override your will.
- Update your emergency contact at work and with your bank.
- Forward mail or make sure the post office knows you moved.
- If you lived together, update your renter's insurance or start your own policy.

## Step 5: Plan for the awkward middle

Some financial ties can't be cut in a day — a lease, a car title, a shared business. For these, make a written agreement with three things: who's responsible for what, the exact dollar amounts, and the deadline. Keep it short and concrete. If the amounts are significant or things turn hostile, a mediator or an attorney is worth the cost — far cheaper than a lawsuit.

## The honest truth

Money tangles after a breakup almost always come from delay, not complexity. Every week you wait to close the joint account or pull them off the card is a week of risk. Give yourself one focused weekend to work this list, and you'll start the next chapter with clean books — which is a gift to yourself and, honestly, to them too.
