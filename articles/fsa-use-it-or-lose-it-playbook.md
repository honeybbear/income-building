# The FSA Use-It-or-Lose-It Playbook: Spend the Balance Before It Vanishes

> Your Flexible Spending Account balance is your own money — but if your plan year ends December 31 and your employer offers no safety net, whatever is left disappears. Here's how to check what you have, what your plan allows, and how to spend it on things you actually need.

Reviewed: October 30, 2026

## Step 1: Find your balance and your deadline

Log into your FSA administrator's portal or app and check two numbers: your remaining balance, and the last day you can incur expenses. Most plans run on the calendar year, so the deadline is December 31 — but some employers use a different plan year, and the deadline is whatever your plan document says, not what you assume.

While you're there, answer the one question that decides everything else: does your plan offer a grace period or a carryover? A plan can offer one of the two, never both, and many offer neither. If it offers a grace period, you get up to two and a half extra months to incur expenses. If it offers a carryover, a portion of your balance rolls into next year. If it offers neither, the balance is gone at midnight on the last day of the plan year.

## Step 2: Know the numbers for 2026

The IRS sets the health FSA rules each year. For 2026, employees can contribute up to $3,400 through salary reduction, and plans that offer a carryover can let you roll up to $680 of unspent money into the next plan year. Your employer can set a lower carryover cap than the IRS maximum, so check your plan's number, not just the federal ceiling. Carried-over money does not reduce next year's contribution limit — it sits on top of whatever you elect for the new year.

One more rule worth knowing: your full annual election is available from day one of the plan year, even before the money has come out of your paychecks. That matters in January, not December — but it explains why the account feels generous early and tight late.

## Step 3: Spend on real needs, in the right order

Do not invent spending just to zero out the balance. But do move up spending you were going to do anyway. Work through this list in order:

- **Prescriptions and refills.** Refill maintenance medications now, and ask your pharmacist whether a 90-day supply is an option. This is the single most useful way to use a leftover balance.
- **Dental and vision.** A cleaning, a filling you've been putting off, new glasses or contact lenses, prescription sunglasses, and contact lens solution are all eligible. Book the appointment now — December appointment books fill up fast.
- **Everyday health supplies.** Sunscreen, first-aid kits, bandages, reading glasses, blood pressure monitors, and thermometers are eligible. Check your administrator's eligible-expense list before buying; the IRS list in Publication 502 is the authority, and your plan follows it.
- **Over-the-counter items.** Since 2020, most over-the-counter medicines and menstrual care products are eligible without a prescription. Pain relievers, allergy medicine, and cold remedies you will genuinely use are fair game.
- **Dependent care, if you have a separate account.** A dependent care FSA is a different account with different rules — for 2026 the household limit is $7,500 ($3,750 if married filing separately). It does not have the same carryover provision, so check its deadline separately.

## Step 4: Watch the traps

- **The run-out clock is separate from the spending clock.** Many plans give you a few extra months to *submit* claims for expenses incurred before the deadline. Submitting late is fine; incurring the expense late is not.
- **Keep receipts.** Your administrator may ask for documentation, especially for larger claims. Snap a photo of every receipt the day you buy.
- **Don't buy in bulk beyond reason.** Stocking up on a year's worth of contact solution you will use is smart. Buying five blood pressure monitors is not — and some administrators flag obviously excessive purchases.
- **Job changes complicate everything.** If you leave your employer, FSA participation generally ends with your employment, and unspent money is typically forfeited. If a job change is coming, spend the balance before your last day, and check whether COBRA continuation of the FSA is an option.

## Step 5: Set next year's election right

The best fix for a leftover balance is not repeating it. Look at what you actually spent this year on eligible expenses, not what you guessed last fall. If you consistently end the year with money left over, lower next year's election. If you ran out by September, raise it — up to the $3,400 limit. And if your plan offers a carryover, that $680 cushion means you can elect a little more aggressively without the same forfeiture risk.

## The honest bottom line

An FSA saves you real money — every dollar goes in pre-tax, skipping income tax and payroll tax — but only if you spend it. The use-it-or-lose-it rule is not a trick; it is the deal you signed up for. Check your balance today, check whether your plan offers a grace period or carryover, and spend the rest on care you actually need before the clock runs out. Forfeiting your own money back to your employer is the one outcome this account never rewards.
