# Gift and Estate Tax Exemption 2027: $20,000 Per Person, $15.48 Million Lifetime

> The IRS just raised the numbers for 2027: you can give $20,000 per recipient tax-free, up from $19,000, and the lifetime estate and gift exemption rises to $15.48 million per person. Here's what changed, who it actually affects, and the December 31 deadline that matters more than any of it.

Reviewed: November 6, 2026

## The new numbers for 2027

The IRS announced its annual inflation adjustments for tax year 2027 on November 5, 2026 (Revenue Procedure 2026-35). The figures that matter for family giving:

| What | 2026 | 2027 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual gift exclusion (per recipient) | $19,000 | **$20,000** |
| Lifetime estate and gift exemption (per person) | $15,000,000 | **$15,480,000** |
| Annual exclusion for gifts to a noncitizen spouse | $194,000 | **$201,000** |

These are tax-year 2027 figures, used on returns filed in 2028. Your 2026 return, filed in spring 2027, still uses the 2026 numbers.

## The $20,000 annual exclusion, in practice

The annual exclusion is the amount one person can give to any other person in a calendar year without filing a gift tax return or paying gift tax. It applies per recipient, so you can give $20,000 to each of your kids, grandkids, and anyone else — every one of them gets the full exclusion.

A married couple doubles it: each spouse has their own $20,000 exclusion, so together they can give **$40,000 to a single recipient** in 2027 with no filing and no tax. Three kids and four grandkids? That's seven recipients times $40,000 — up to $280,000 moved in one year, entirely within the exclusion.

And the exclusion never counts against your lifetime exemption. The $20,000-per-person gifts are free and clear; the $15.48 million only comes into play when you give more than the annual amount to one person in a year.

## The $15.48 million lifetime exemption — who it actually affects

The lifetime exemption is the total amount one person can give away during life or leave at death before federal estate or gift tax applies. For a married couple, that's **$30.96 million combined** in 2027.

Be honest about who this touches: very few families. The federal estate tax only applies above the exemption, and the vast majority of estates fall far below $15 million. But the number matters for planning: the previous exemption was scheduled to drop by roughly half after 2025, and the tax law signed in 2025 locked in a $15 million base instead, with inflation adjustments pushing it up from there. Families planning large transfers no longer have to race a cliff.

## The deadline that matters more: December 31, 2026

The 2027 numbers are nice, but the date that should be on your calendar is December 31, 2026. The annual exclusion is use-it-or-lose-it — it resets every January 1 and never carries forward.

If you planned to help an adult child with a down payment or fund a grandchild's account this year, a gift completed by December 31 counts against 2026's $19,000 exclusion. Wait until January and it counts against 2027's $20,000 instead. Either way the gift works — but if you want to use *both* years' exclusions for the same recipient, the December gift has to actually be complete before the year ends.

## What if you give more than $20,000?

Giving more than the exclusion to one person doesn't mean you owe tax — it means you **file IRS Form 709** (the gift tax return) by the following April 15. In most cases the form just tracks the excess against your lifetime exemption. You only owe actual gift tax once your total lifetime gifts eat through the full $15.48 million.

Two things that don't count toward the exclusion at all, in any year:

- **Tuition paid directly to the school** — unlimited, no filing, no effect on your $20,000.
- **Medical bills paid directly to the provider** — same deal.

Paying a grandchild's college tuition by sending the check straight to the university doesn't touch your annual exclusion. Writing the same check to the grandchild's bank account does.

## Two other 2027 numbers worth knowing

The same IRS announcement moved a few figures most households will feel more directly:

- **Standard deduction:** $33,200 for married couples filing jointly (up $1,000) and $16,600 for single filers.
- **Child tax credit:** $2,300 per qualifying child (up $100), with the refundable portion up to $1,800.

Tax brackets also rise about 3.2% for inflation while the seven rates stay the same, from 10% to 37%.

## Practical checklist

1. **Use the 2026 exclusion before it expires** — finish any planned 2026 gifts by December 31.
2. **Plan 2027 gifts at $20,000 per recipient per donor** ($40,000 from a couple) to stay paperwork-free.
3. **Pay tuition or medical costs directly to the institution** when that's the goal — it bypasses the limit entirely.
4. **If you exceed the annual amount**, plan to file Form 709 by the following April 15 — and don't panic; for almost everyone it's reporting only.
5. **Check your state's rules** — the federal exemption doesn't cover state estate or inheritance taxes.

The annual exclusion is one of the few tax-planning tools that costs nothing to use. The only requirement is timing: once December 31 passes, that year's allowance is gone for good.
