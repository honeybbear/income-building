# The Hidden $1,500-a-Year Threat to Your Retirement: GLP-1 Drugs

> Weight-loss drugs are helping millions of Americans feel healthier — and quietly eating the money meant for retirement. Here's the tradeoff in real numbers, and how to handle it without giving up either one.

Reviewed: November 8, 2026

## The number that started the conversation

A USA Today report this week put a price on a tradeoff millions of Americans are making quietly: the median GLP-1 user now spends more than $1,500 a year out of pocket on the drugs, according to a Virginia Commonwealth University study — and nearly 1 in 5 users spends more than 10% of their household income on them.

GLP-1 drugs — the class used for weight loss and diabetes — work. That's the complicated part. People feel better, move more, and expect to live longer. But for many, the medication is a cost that never ends: most people who stop taking it regain weight, so the bill is effectively for life. At $1,500 a year, that's $15,000 over a decade — money that, in a retirement account, would have been compounding the whole time.

## How the tradeoff shows up in real budgets

The Nationwide Retirement Institute's 2026 Healthcare Costs Survey of 1,933 adults found the squeeze is already happening: half of GLP-1 users worry they may have to choose between the medication and saving enough for retirement, and 24% say keeping access to the medication matters more than increasing retirement savings.

More than half of current users — 54% — had already changed their financial behavior because of the price:

- **17% took on debt**, such as credit cards or loans, to pay for it
- **14% withdrew money** from savings, investments, or retirement accounts
- **14% reduced retirement contributions** or investments
- **10% delayed or avoided other medical care**

Borrowing or raiding a retirement account to pay for a recurring prescription is the most expensive way to fund it — card interest compounds against you, and a withdrawal can trigger taxes and penalties while removing decades of growth.

## The optimism trap

The survey found a second, subtler problem: feeling healthier is making many people save *less*. 88% of users said the drugs make them more optimistic about their long-term health. 64% said they may retire later, 59% feel less pressure to increase retirement savings, and 57% are less concerned about needing long-term care insurance.

Feeling healthier is real. But as Kristi Rodriguez of the Nationwide Retirement Institute put it, living longer and healthier also means needing *more* money — for a longer retirement, and to keep buying the medication itself. Nearly 60% of users worry about affording GLP-1 medication in retirement. Optimism about health is not a retirement plan; it's a reason the plan needs to be bigger.

## Step 1: Fund the employer match first

If your job offers a 401(k) match, that match is the one guaranteed return in personal finance. Patrick Simasko, an elder law attorney and financial adviser quoted in the report, gave the ordering plainly: fully fund a retirement account every year, contribute as much as possible to capture the employer match, automate contributions from your pay — and only then budget whatever is left for GLP-1.

A match you skip to pay for a prescription is free money from your employer, gone forever. If the math genuinely doesn't stretch to both, talk to your doctor about alternatives — don't quietly cut the 401(k).

## Step 2: Price the drug like a bill, not a splurge

A recurring medication belongs in the budget as a fixed line item, next to rent and insurance — not a flexible expense paid from whatever is left. That means:

- **Find out the real price for you.** Out-of-pocket costs vary widely by insurer and plan. Check your plan's formulary and any manufacturer savings programs before assuming the sticker price is your price.
- **Put the monthly amount in the budget first.** If the drug costs $125 a month, that's $125 that isn't available for dining out or a subscription — not $125 taken from the retirement contribution.
- **Re-check coverage every open enrollment.** Drug coverage changes year to year — open enrollment is the moment to compare plans on what they actually pay for your prescriptions, not just the premium.

## Step 3: Run the long math

Before letting a monthly drug cost quietly shrink your savings rate, do the simple version of the calculation: what does that monthly amount become over 10 or 20 years if it went into a retirement account instead? A $125 monthly cost is $1,500 a year — $30,000 over 20 years before any growth. Seeing the number makes the decision conscious instead of automatic.

Then plan the other direction too: if you expect to take the medication for life, your retirement has to fund it as well — so your target is bigger, not smaller, than a no-medication retirement. A longer, healthier life costs more, not less.

## Step 4: Don't raid the retirement account to pay for it

Withdrawing from a 401(k) or IRA to cover a recurring cost hurts twice: you lose the money and its decades of growth, and you may owe income tax plus a penalty on top. If the prescription strains the budget, the cheaper levers are cutting discretionary spending, asking your doctor or pharmacist about lower-cost alternatives, and checking whether your insurance plan is the right one — not the retirement account.

## The one-line version

Treat the drug as a permanent bill, fund the employer match before anything else, and never pay for today's prescription with tomorrow's retirement money. The medication may help you live longer — make sure your savings are built for the longer life it gives you.
