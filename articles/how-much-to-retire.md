# How Much Do You Actually Need to Retire? Forget the Headline Number

> You've probably seen headlines claiming Americans think they need millions to retire. That number is useless to you personally — because your retirement number depends on your spending, not on a survey average. Here's how to estimate your own number in about 20 minutes.

Reviewed: November 2, 2026

## Why the headline number misleads you

A single "magic number" for retirement ignores everything that actually determines what you need: how much you spend each year, whether your mortgage will be paid off, how much Social Security will cover, where you live, and when you stop working. Two people with identical savings can have completely different retirement realities — one owns a paid-off house in a low-cost town and spends $35,000 a year, the other rents in an expensive city and spends $80,000.

So ignore the headline. Build your number from your own life, in three steps.

## Step 1: Estimate what you'll spend in a year of retirement

Start with what you spend now, then adjust. Some costs fall in retirement: no more commuting, no more saving for retirement itself, possibly a paid-off mortgage. Some rise: health care is the big one, and travel or hobbies often spike in the early retirement years.

A practical way to do it: take your current annual spending and subtract the costs that will genuinely disappear. Then add a line for health care and one for the lifestyle you actually want — not a fantasy, your real plans. A range is fine: a low estimate and a comfortable estimate. Most people land somewhere between 70% and 90% of their pre-retirement spending, but your situation beats any rule of thumb.

## Step 2: Subtract the income you don't have to save for

Not every dollar of retirement spending has to come from your savings. Subtract income that arrives on its own:

- **Social Security.** Create an account at ssa.gov and check your official estimate — don't guess. The age you claim matters enormously: claiming at 62 permanently reduces your monthly benefit versus waiting until full retirement age or 70. If you're married, your spouse's benefit counts too.
- **A pension**, if you have one, including any military or government pension.
- **Rental income or part-time work** you realistically plan to continue.

What's left is the gap your savings must fill each year. This is the number that matters.

## Step 3: Turn the annual gap into a savings target

The common rule of thumb is the 25x rule: multiply your annual gap by 25. It comes from the idea that you can withdraw roughly 4% of a balanced portfolio per year. It's a rough planning tool, not a guarantee — but it turns an abstract worry into a concrete target.

Here's a worked example, using round numbers to show the math:

- Retirement spending: $50,000 per year
- Social Security plus a small pension: $25,000 per year
- Gap to cover from savings: $25,000 per year
- Rough target: $25,000 × 25 = $625,000

Change one input and watch how much the target moves. If Social Security covers $30,000 instead of $25,000, the target drops to $500,000. If spending is $60,000 instead of $50,000, it jumps to $875,000. That sensitivity is the whole point: the levers that shrink your target are spending less, working a bit longer, and claiming Social Security later — not finding a magic investment.

## What moves your number the most

- **A paid-off home.** Housing is usually the largest retirement expense. Eliminating a mortgage or rent payment can cut your annual gap more than years of extra saving.
- **Health care.** Budget for it separately and honestly. Medicare covers a lot but not everything — premiums, dental, vision, hearing, and long-term care can all sit outside it. If you retire before 65, price marketplace health insurance for the gap years; it is often the single biggest early-retirement cost.
- **When you stop working.** Each extra year of work does three things at once: one more year of saving, one less year of spending from savings, and a larger Social Security benefit. It is the most powerful lever most people have, and it's worth weighing against the years of freedom you'd give up.
- **Where you live.** Moving from a high-cost area to a lower-cost one can cut your annual spending by more than any budgeting trick. It's a big life decision, not a spreadsheet tweak — but it belongs in the conversation.

## If the number looks impossible, you're not done — you're just starting

Most people who run this math for the first time get a target bigger than their current savings. That's normal, not a verdict — it just tells you which lever to pull:

- **Save more, automatically.** Raise your workplace retirement contribution by one percentage point now, and schedule another point in six months. You adjust to each step and barely notice it.
- **Delay Social Security if you can.** For many people, waiting from 62 to 70 raises the monthly benefit substantially — it's one of the best "returns" available, and it requires no market risk.
- **Plan a phased retirement.** Part-time work for a few years, even at modest pay, can bridge the gap between stopping full-time work and claiming full benefits.
- **Revisit the spending side.** The retirement you can afford is the one your spending allows. Cutting $5,000 a year from retirement spending cuts your rough target by $125,000. That's a powerful trade.

## Your 20-minute homework

1. Write down your estimated annual retirement spending — a low number and a comfortable number.
2. Look up your Social Security estimate at ssa.gov and add any pension.
3. Subtract to find your gap, multiply by 25, and write the target down.
4. Compare it to what you have saved. The difference is your plan, not your failure — it tells you exactly which lever to pull next.

Your spending, your benefits, and your gap can. Run your own math, revisit it once a year, and adjust one lever at a time.
