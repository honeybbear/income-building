# The Inherited IRA Playbook: The 10-Year Clock and How to Keep the Tax Bill Down

> Inheriting an IRA can feel like a windfall — until the tax bill arrives. Since the SECURE Act, most non-spouse heirs must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, and withdrawals from a traditional IRA are taxed as ordinary income. A little planning can keep that bill much smaller.

Reviewed: October 28, 2026

## First: what kind of heir are you?

The rules split sharply on one question — were you the spouse?

- **A surviving spouse** has the most options: treat the IRA as your own, roll it into your own IRA, or remain a beneficiary and take distributions on your own schedule. Rolling it into your own IRA is usually the simplest move, because it restarts the normal rules (including delaying required distributions until your own required beginning date).
- **Everyone else** is a non-spouse beneficiary, and the 10-year rule below is your clock — unless you qualify as an "eligible designated beneficiary" (see below).

One hard rule for non-spouses: you **cannot** roll an inherited IRA into your own IRA or 401(k). It must stay titled as an inherited IRA, in the original owner's name for your benefit. Moving it wrong can turn the whole balance into a taxable distribution, so keep the inherited title intact and let the custodian handle transfers directly.

## The 10-year rule, in plain English

For most non-spouse heirs who inherited after 2019, the account must be **fully emptied by December 31 of the 10th year after the year of death**. There is no requirement to take anything out in years 1 through 9 — unless the original owner had already started taking required minimum distributions (RMDs). In that case, IRS final regulations require you to keep taking annual RMDs during years 1–9 as well, with the remainder out by year 10.

The exceptions — "eligible designated beneficiaries" — get the old stretch rules instead:

- A minor child of the account owner (the 10-year clock starts when they reach adulthood)
- A disabled or chronically ill beneficiary
- Someone not more than 10 years younger than the deceased

If you fall in one of these categories, you can generally stretch distributions over your own life expectancy. Confirm your status with the custodian early — it changes everything about the plan.

## Why the tax bill surprises people

Withdrawals from an inherited **traditional** IRA are taxed as ordinary income in the year you take them. That creates two traps:

- **The lump-sum trap.** Cashing out a large IRA in one year stacks the entire balance on top of your salary in a single tax year, potentially pushing you into a higher bracket. The same dollars spread over several years can be taxed at much lower rates.
- **The year-10 trap.** The opposite mistake: taking nothing for nine years, then emptying the whole account in year 10. Same problem, same bracket spike.

An inherited **Roth** IRA follows the same 10-year emptying deadline, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free — so the timing matters far less for taxes, though you still must empty it on schedule.

## A practical withdrawal plan

**Step 1: Get the facts in the first months.** Confirm the account type (traditional vs. Roth), whether the original owner had started RMDs, and whether a required distribution is still due for the year of death — if one was missed, it still needs to come out. Note the deadline year on your calendar now, not in year 9.

**Step 2: Sketch the 10-year spread.** As a starting point, divide the balance by the years remaining and consider taking roughly that amount each year. Then adjust: take more in low-income years (a gap year, a sabbatical, early retirement before Social Security starts) and less in high-income years (a big bonus year, a working spouse's peak earnings).

**Step 3: Watch the bracket, not just the balance.** The goal isn't to empty the account as fast as possible — it's to keep each year's withdrawal in the lowest bracket you can manage. Even rough bracket awareness beats autopilot: a withdrawal that fills up a lower bracket this year is cheaper than the same dollars stacked on top of a high salary later.

**Step 4: Don't forget state taxes and side effects.** A big withdrawal year can also raise state income tax, Medicare premiums (through IRMAA two years later), and the tax on Social Security benefits. If you're near retirement, run the plan past a tax professional before committing to it.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Missing the year-of-death RMD.** If the deceased hadn't yet taken that year's required distribution, the beneficiary must take it.
- **Taking the lump sum in grief.** No deadline forces a fast decision in year 1 (beyond any year-of-death RMD). Give yourself time to plan.
- **Ignoring a Roth inherited IRA.** It still has a 10-year deadline, and missing it triggers penalties.
- **Naming no successor beneficiary.** If you die before emptying the account, your own heirs inherit the remaining clock.

## The bottom line

An inherited IRA rewards patience and punishes lump sums. Know which clock you're on, spread traditional IRA withdrawals across lower-income years, and get the year-of-death and RMD details right in year one. The tax code gives you up to a decade to get this right — use all of it, deliberately.
