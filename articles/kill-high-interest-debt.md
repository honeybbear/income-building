# Killing High-Interest Debt: Avalanche vs. Snowball

> High-interest debt is an emergency. Here are the two proven payoff strategies — pick the one that fits your personality and execute it relentlessly.

Reviewed: October 6, 2026

## Face the full picture first

You can't beat what you won't look at. List every debt: creditor, balance, interest rate, and minimum payment. Credit cards, personal loans, medical debt in collections, buy-now-pay-later balances — all of it. This list is your battle map. Most people find this step uncomfortable and also weirdly motivating, because now the problem has a defined size.

## The two strategies

There are two widely used payoff methods. Both work. The difference is psychology.

**The avalanche (highest interest first).** Order your debts by interest rate, highest to lowest. Pay minimums on everything, then throw every extra dollar at the highest-rate debt. When it's gone, roll that payment into the next-highest. Mathematically, this is the cheapest and fastest way out — you minimize total interest paid.

**The snowball (smallest balance first).** Order your debts by balance, smallest to largest, ignoring interest rates. Attack the smallest first. You pay slightly more interest overall, but you get your first "paid in full" win faster — and for many people, that momentum is the difference between sticking with the plan and quitting.

**Which to choose?** If you're analytical and motivated by efficiency, take the avalanche. If you've tried to get out of debt before and stalled, take the snowball — quick wins keep you going. The best strategy is the one you'll actually follow for months.

## The non-negotiable rules

- **Stop adding new debt.** Put the credit cards away — literally, in a drawer or frozen in ice if you have to. You can't fill a bucket that has a hole in it.
- **Pay all minimums, every month.** Late fees and penalty interest rates will wreck your progress. Automate minimums so this happens without willpower.
- **Direct every extra dollar at the target debt.** Side income, tax refunds, money from selling stuff — it all goes to the current target until it's dead.

## Tactics that can help

**Call and ask for a lower rate.** It sounds too simple, but calling your card issuer and asking for a rate reduction sometimes works, especially if you've been a customer for a while and have a decent payment history. The worst they can say is no.

**Consider a balance transfer carefully.** Some cards offer 0% introductory rates on transferred balances. This can save real money — but read the terms: there's usually a transfer fee (often 3–5%), the rate expires, and any new purchases may accrue interest immediately. Only do this if you have a plan to pay the balance before the promotional period ends.

**Don't raid retirement to pay debt.** Cashing out a 401(k) triggers taxes and penalties and robs your future self. The math almost never works in your favor.

## What about low-interest debt?

Not all debt is an emergency. A mortgage at a low fixed rate or a federal student loan at a modest rate is very different from a credit card at 24%. Once high-interest debt is gone, you can decide whether to aggressively pay down lower-rate debt or redirect that money toward saving and investing — there's reasonable disagreement among experts, and either choice is defensible.
