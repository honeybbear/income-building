# The Lost 401(k) Playbook: Reclaim the Retirement Money You Forgot

> Every job change is a chance to leave a retirement account behind. Old 401(k)s don't disappear — they're still yours, protected by federal law. Here's exactly how to find them and what to do once you do.

Reviewed: October 21, 2026

## Why accounts get lost

Retirement accounts go missing for ordinary reasons: you moved, changed your email, the company merged or rebranded, or you assumed a small balance wasn't worth chasing. It always is — your own contributions are yours regardless of vesting, and even a few thousand dollars left growing for decades matters.

When a company disappears entirely, the money still isn't gone. Federal law requires old plan assets to be accounted for, and there are now free government tools built specifically to help you search.

## Step 1: Reconstruct your work history

Make a list of every employer you've worked for, including part-time and short-stint jobs. For each one, dig for any of these:

- Old 401(k) statements (paper or email — search your inbox for the plan name)
- The name of the company that ran the plan (the "recordkeeper")
- Tax forms from those years — retirement activity sometimes shows up there

Even one document naming the recordkeeper is usually enough: log in there or call them and they'll find your account by name and Social Security number.

## Step 2: Call your former employer

The fastest path is often the most direct. Call the former employer's HR or benefits office and ask which company administers the 401(k) plan and whether you still have a balance. If the company merged, rebranded, or closed, call the successor company — retirement plan obligations transfer with the business.

**Ask specifically where small balances went.** Plans routinely move former workers' small balances somewhere else rather than leaving them in the plan, so if the plan says your balance is zero, your money may have been transferred — not lost.

## Step 3: Search the free databases

If the phone route fails, work through these free search tools. Searching all of them takes under an hour:

- **Department of Labor Retirement Savings Lost and Found** — lostandfound.dol.gov. Created under the SECURE 2.0 Act and launched in late 2024, this free federal tool checks your Social Security number against private-sector retirement plans and shows you the plan administrator's contact information. It requires verifying your identity through Login.gov (government photo ID, Social Security number, and a mobile device). Honest limits: it shows plans you participated in, not guaranteed balances; coverage is still filling in; and it doesn't cover IRAs, government-employee plans, or some religious-organization plans.
- **National Registry of Unclaimed Retirement Benefits** — unclaimedretirementbenefits.com. A free registry where many employers register accounts of former workers. Search by Social Security number; results come back in seconds. Not all employers participate, so an empty result isn't conclusive.
- **State unclaimed property** — missingmoney.com searches many states at once by name and prior addresses. Employers report unclaimed accounts to the state when contact fails.
- **PBGC pension search** — pbgc.gov, for old-style pension plans from companies that closed or went bankrupt. Search with your last name and the last four digits of your Social Security number.

One caution that deserves emphasis: because the real federal tool requires identity verification, scammers love this topic. The address is lostandfound.dol.gov, and the Labor Department never charges a fee to recover funds and never asks for your password or one-time codes. Anyone who contacts you first about "found" retirement money, or who asks for a fee to release it, is not real.

## Step 4: Know the small-balance rules

What happens to your balance depends on its size, under SECURE 2.0:

- **Above $7,000:** the old plan generally must let you leave the money there if you want.
- **$1,000 to $7,000:** the plan may automatically roll it into an IRA in your name at a provider they choose — check any IRA mailings you've ignored.
- **Under $1,000:** the plan may simply mail a check to your last known address. If you've moved, that's how an account quietly vanishes.

If a check was mailed and never cashed, it likely ended up in state unclaimed property — which is why the missingmoney.com step above is worth the five minutes.

## Step 5: Decide what to do with found money

Once you've located an account, you have four basic options:

- **Leave it in the old plan** (allowed for balances above $7,000). Fine if the fees are low and investments are decent — but you'll have one more account to track forever.
- **Roll it into a traditional IRA.** Keeps the tax-deferred status, and you control the investments and fees.
- **Roll it into your current employer's plan.** Convenient if the new plan has good options — one fewer account, one statement.
- **Cash it out.** This is almost always the worst option. The payout is taxed as income in the year you take it, and if you're under 59½ there's generally a 10% early-withdrawal penalty on top. A 30-year-old cashing out a small balance is trading decades of compounding for a fraction of the account's value today.

If the money has been growing while you weren't watching, treat the rollover itself carefully: request a **direct rollover** (money goes straight from the old plan to the new account) so no taxes are withheld and no deadlines are triggered.

## The bottom line

Start with your work-history list, then call former employers, then run the free databases. Update your contact information with every plan once you find them so it never happens again — and every time you change jobs from now on, make a rollover decision before the paperwork gets cold. The best time to find a lost 401(k) was years ago; the second-best time is this afternoon.
