# The Open Enrollment Playbook: Pick the Right Health Plan Before the Deadline

> Open enrollment is the one window each year when you can switch health plans or change your elections without a life event. Most employer plans run it in October or November. Here's how to decide in under an hour — and the mistakes that cost people real money.

Reviewed: October 18, 2026

## Step 1: Know your deadline — it isn't flexible

Employer plans usually give you a two- to four-week window in the fall. If you miss it, your current elections roll over automatically, and you're stuck with them until the next life event (marriage, new child, job change) or the next open enrollment. Mark the close date on your calendar the day the HR email arrives, and aim to finish a week early — enrollment portals slow down or glitch on the last day.

On the ACA Marketplace, open enrollment generally runs November 1 to January 15 in most states. The same "no changes outside the window" rule applies unless you qualify for a special enrollment period.

## Step 2: Price the whole year, not the premium

The monthly premium is the most visible number and the most misleading one. The number that actually matters is your **total annual cost**: premiums for 12 months, plus what you'd pay out of pocket based on how much care you use. Work through three scenarios:

- **Low year** (checkups and one or two sick visits): premium + copays.
- **Medium year** (a few specialist visits, some prescriptions): premium + copays + part of your deductible.
- **Bad year** (surgery, ER visit, new diagnosis): premium + the plan's out-of-pocket maximum.

A cheap premium with a high out-of-pocket maximum is often the most expensive option in the bad-year scenario — exactly when you can least afford it.

## Step 3: Compare the plan types by your real life, not the brochure

Most employers offer some combination of these:

- **PPO**: higher premium, lower deductible, you can see specialists without referrals and go out of network (at higher cost). Best if you have ongoing conditions, a preferred specialist, or hate gatekeepers.
- **HMO/EPO**: lower premium, must use in-network providers; HMOs require referrals from a primary care doctor. Best if your doctors are in network and you like predictable copays.
- **HDHP (High-Deductible Health Plan)**: lowest premium, highest deductible — but it's the only plan that lets you open an HSA. Best if you're relatively healthy and can afford to fund the HSA (see Step 5).

The deciding question isn't "which plan is best" — it's "which plan is best for *my* doctors, *my* prescriptions, and *my* health pattern." Check every plan's provider directory for your doctors and its drug formulary for your prescriptions. Networks change year to year; a doctor who was in network last year may not be this year.

## Step 4: Elect your supplemental benefits deliberately

Open enrollment isn't just health insurance. Walk through each of these:

- **Dental and vision**: usually worth it if you have regular cleanings, kids who need orthodontics, or you replace glasses annually. Compare the annual premium against what you'd pay out of pocket — sometimes the employer subsidizes enough to make it a bargain; sometimes you're paying more than the care costs.
- **Life and disability insurance**: basic employer coverage is often free — take it. Consider whether to buy supplemental coverage, especially disability insurance if your family depends on your income. This is the one election people most under-buy.
- **Dependent care FSA**: if you pay for daycare, after-school care, or elder care, this lets you pay with pre-tax dollars — a genuine tax discount on money you're spending anyway. Note it has the same use-it-or-lose-it rules as a health FSA.
- **Wellness incentives**: many plans offer credits for a biometric screening, an annual physical, or completing a health assessment. These are essentially free money for things you should do anyway.

## Step 5: Decide about the HSA vs. FSA

If you choose the HDHP, you can open a **Health Savings Account** — contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free. The balance is yours forever and rolls over year after year, so funding it is closer to saving than spending. If you can cash-flow the deductible from the HSA, it's one of the best tax-advantaged accounts available to ordinary workers.

If you choose a PPO or HMO instead, you'll likely be offered a **health FSA** — pre-tax dollars for medical expenses, but mostly use-it-or-lose-it by year end (some plans allow a grace period or a small rollover). The FSA is worth electing if you can predict real expenses: a planned dental crown, prescription glasses, orthodontia installments.

Do the simple version: estimate next year's predictable medical costs, and elect that amount — no more — in the FSA. Over-electing means donating money back to your employer.

## Step 6: Update your beneficiaries and emergency info

Open enrollment is also the natural moment to check that your life-insurance and 401(k) beneficiaries still match your intentions. A beneficiary form from before a marriage, divorce, or child is a common and painful surprise for families. It takes two minutes.

## Step 7: Submit, screenshot, and confirm

After you click submit, save or screenshot the confirmation page and watch for a confirmation email. Then check your first January paycheck to verify the right deductions are being taken — payroll errors are far easier to fix in January than in April. If something looks wrong, contact HR immediately; there are strict deadlines for correcting enrollment mistakes.

## The honest bottom line

Nobody loves spending an evening comparing health plans. But your elections lock in for a full year, and the difference between the right and wrong plan can be thousands of dollars — money that comes straight out of your budget either way. Spend the hour. Your January self will thank you.

*This is educational content, not financial advice. Insurance and tax rules vary by employer and state — confirm the specifics of your plan documents and consider professional guidance for major decisions.*
