# Private Assets Are Coming to 401(k)s — Here's What to Check Before You Buy In

> Headlines say retirement savers are keen to invest in private assets, and regulators have opened the door for 401(k) plans to offer them. Before you put retirement money into something you can't sell on a normal trading day, here's what private assets actually are, why they're riskier than they look in a brochure, and the checklist to run first.

Reviewed: November 3, 2026

## What "private assets" means in a retirement plan

Private assets are investments that aren't traded on a public stock exchange. The main types showing up in retirement-plan conversations are:

- **Private equity** — funds that buy whole companies, improve them, and sell them years later
- **Private credit** — loans to companies, made outside the public bond market
- **Private real estate** — funds that own buildings, warehouses, or apartments directly

In August 2025, an executive order directed federal regulators to review the rules that had mostly kept these assets out of 401(k) plans. Plan providers have been rolling out options since, often tucked inside target-date funds rather than offered as a standalone choice. That means some workers may get exposure without ever picking it themselves — which makes reading your plan's disclosures worth doing.

## The pitch — and why it's only half the story

The sales case is straightforward: private markets are far larger than public markets by number of companies, so excluding them means missing opportunities, and their returns haven't always moved in lockstep with stocks. Both points have some truth to them.

But the pitch usually skips the parts that matter most to an ordinary saver:

1. **You can't sell when you want to.** A stock fund lets you move money any business day. Private funds typically lock money up for months or years, with redemptions only on set dates — and sometimes gated, meaning the fund can delay your withdrawal when many investors want out at once. In a retirement account you may need to rebalance, change jobs, or take a distribution, and an illiquid holding complicates all of that.

2. **Fees are higher, and harder to see.** A broad index fund can cost a tiny fraction of a percent a year. Private funds charge management fees plus a share of profits, and the layers can stack: the fund's fee, the plan's fee, and the fee of any target-date fund wrapping it. Ask for the all-in number, not the headline rate.

3. **The price is an estimate, not a market quote.** Public stocks are priced every second by millions of trades. Private holdings are valued periodically by the fund manager or an appraiser, using models. That smooths the ride on paper — values look steadier than stocks — but it doesn't mean the risk went away. It means you see it later, and sometimes all at once when a valuation is revised.

4. **Performance figures are hard to compare.** Private funds report returns over long, irregular periods, and the best-known funds are often closed to new money. An average return quoted for the industry may reflect funds you could never have bought.

## The checklist before you invest a dollar

If your plan offers a private-asset option, run through these five questions:

- **How much of my account would this be?** A small sleeve — a single-digit percentage — inside a diversified fund is a different decision from a large standalone allocation. If you can't find the percentage, that's a warning sign on its own.
- **What are the total fees, all layers included?** Get the number in writing from the fund fact sheet and your plan's fee disclosure, and compare it to what you pay for your index funds today.
- **When can I get out, and what could delay it?** Look for lockup periods, redemption windows, and gating language. If you might need the money for a job change or an early retirement, illiquidity is a real cost.
- **How is it valued, and how often?** Quarterly manager estimates are normal for this asset class — but understand that the number on your statement is a model output, not a sale price.
- **What does it replace?** If the money comes out of your stock and bond index funds, you're trading something cheap, liquid, and transparent for something expensive, locked-up, and opaque. The burden of proof is on the new holding, not the old one.

## The honest default

For most workers, doing nothing here is a perfectly good decision. A low-cost mix of stock and bond index funds, held for decades, has built most ordinary retirements — and none of its advantages (daily liquidity, visible prices, tiny fees) require trusting a manager's valuation model.

Private assets may make sense for some savers with long horizons, high risk tolerance, and a plan option with transparent fees and a genuinely small allocation. But "available in your 401(k)" is not the same as "right for your 401(k)," and enthusiasm in headlines is not evidence. If you do opt in, keep it small, know the exit terms before you enter, and revisit the decision every year when your plan's disclosures arrive — because the fund you joined and the fund you hold in five years may not be the same fund at all.
