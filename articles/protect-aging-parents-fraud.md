# Protecting Aging Parents From Fraud: A Family Checklist That Actually Works

> Reported fraud losses among older Americans hit $3 billion in 2025, according to an FTC report covered by CNBC — with losses of at least six figures surging. Most of that money never comes back. This is the family conversation and checklist that can stop it before it starts.

Reviewed: October 2026

## Step 1: Have the conversation before there's a crisis

The hardest part of fraud protection isn't the technology — it's the talk. Many older adults are embarrassed to admit they got a suspicious call, and scammers count on that silence. Bring it up calmly, as a team effort rather than a lecture: "Scammers are targeting everyone right now, and I want us to have a plan together."

Agree on one simple family rule up front: **no financial decision happens under pressure.** Any caller who demands secrecy, urgency, or payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency is a scammer — full stop. Real banks, government agencies, and tech companies never ask for payment that way. If your parent can repeat that rule back to you, you've already done the most important work.

## Step 2: Lock down the accounts that matter most

A few free, high-impact moves protect more than any antivirus software:

- **Freeze credit at all three bureaus.** A credit freeze (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) blocks new accounts from being opened in your parent's name. It's free, it doesn't affect their credit score, and it can be temporarily lifted when they legitimately need credit. This single step stops most identity-theft damage cold.
- **Turn on transaction alerts.** Set up text or email alerts for every charge over a small threshold, plus alerts for new payees, password changes, and large withdrawals. Alerts turn a scam discovered months later into a scam discovered in minutes.
- **Add a trusted contact to brokerage and bank accounts.** Most major brokerages and banks let an account holder name a trusted contact — someone the firm can call if it spots unusual activity or worries about exploitation. It doesn't give you access to the money; it gives the institution a second pair of eyes.
- **Use strong, unique passwords and two-factor authentication.** A password manager removes the excuse for reusing passwords, and app-based two-factor beats text-message codes when available.

## Step 3: Shrink the attack surface

Scammers reach people through the phone, email, and mailbox. Reduce all three:

- **Register numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry** (donotcall.gov). It won't stop criminals, but it cuts legitimate telemarketing noise so a scam call stands out.
- **Set phones to silence unknown callers.** Both iPhone and Android can send unknown numbers straight to voicemail. Legitimate callers leave a message; scammers usually don't.
- **Opt out of pre-approved credit offers** at optoutprescreen.com. Fewer offers in the mailbox means fewer chances for mail theft and fewer tempting "you've been approved" letters.
- **Go paperless where possible, and shred the rest.** Old bank statements, tax documents, and medical bills in the trash are a goldmine for identity thieves.

## Step 4: Know the scams targeting older adults right now

The playbook rarely changes, only the details:

- **The grandparent scam:** "Grandma, it's me, I'm in trouble and need money wired now — don't tell Mom and Dad." Agree on a family safe word that a real grandchild would know.
- **Government impersonation:** Callers claiming to be from the IRS, Social Security, or Medicare demanding payment or personal information. Real agencies contact people by mail first and never threaten immediate arrest.
- **Tech support scams:** A pop-up or call claiming the computer has a virus, asking for remote access or payment. Never let a stranger control the computer.
- **Romance and investment scams:** New online relationships that quickly turn to requests for money, or "guaranteed" crypto and investment opportunities. Guaranteed high returns with no risk do not exist.
- **The "your account is compromised" trick:** A caller or email urging immediate action to "protect" an account — and then asking for the password or a verification code. Never share a code with anyone who calls you.

## Step 5: If money already went out, act in the first hour

Speed matters enormously, and shame must not slow it down:

1. **Call the bank or card issuer immediately** and ask to freeze or reverse the transaction. Wire transfers and gift cards are hardest to recover; credit card charges are the most reversible.
2. **Report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov** and to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov.
3. **File a police report** — banks and credit bureaus often require one for fraud claims.
4. **Place a fraud alert** with one credit bureau (it notifies the other two) and review credit reports at annualcreditreport.com.
5. **Tell the family.** Other relatives may have received the same pitch, and warning them could save them too.

## Step 6: Make it a routine, not a one-time talk

Set a standing monthly check-in: review statements together, confirm no new accounts or payees appeared, and ask about any odd calls or messages. Frame it as ordinary household maintenance, like checking smoke detectors — not as surveillance. The families that avoid fraud losses aren't the ones with the fanciest software; they're the ones where a suspicious call gets discussed at dinner the same day it happens.
