# Dave Ramsey Says You Can Withdraw 12% and Never Touch Your Principal. Here's the Catch

> Dave Ramsey told a caller this week that withdrawing less than 12% a year means you'll "never touch" your retirement principal. The arithmetic sounds clean. The history says otherwise — and the difference is worth about half your retirement income.

Reviewed: November 9, 2026

## The claim, in Ramsey's own math

The story making the rounds this week (24/7 Wall St., November 6, 2026) comes from The Ramsey Show, where Ramsey told a caller she could withdraw aggressively from her retirement savings without ever eating into the principal. His reasoning has been consistent for years, and it goes like this:

- Good growth-stock mutual funds average about 12% a year.
- Inflation runs about 4% a year.
- So if you withdraw 8% — the 12% return minus 4% inflation — you live on the growth, the principal stays whole, and in Ramsey's words to one caller, your money is "mathematically infinite."

His written guidance at Ramsey Solutions works the same example: a $1 million portfolio, an 11% return, 3% inflation, and an 8% withdrawal rate — $80,000 a year, forever. Compared with the famous 4% rule, that's double the income from the same nest egg. No wonder people want it to be true.

## Problem 1: 12% is an average, not a schedule

The 12% figure comes from the long-run average return of the S&P 500 — Ramsey Solutions cites an 11.86% average from 1928 through 2025. But an average is not what your account actually earns year to year. Some years the market falls 20% or more; other years it rises 30%. The average smooths all of that away, and your retirement can't live on a smoothed average.

Here's the simplest way to see the trap. Imagine your $1 million falls 20% in your first year of retirement, to $800,000, and you still withdraw your $80,000. You're now at $720,000. Even if the market then returns its "average" 12% the next year, you're at about $806,000 — still far below where you started, and still withdrawing $80,000 a year. A bad year early in retirement does damage that good years can't easily undo, because you're selling investments at the bottom to fund spending. Planners call this sequence-of-returns risk, and it's the single biggest reason a high withdrawal rate fails.

Ramsey's math also quietly uses an arithmetic average rather than the compound return you actually experience. The market's long-run compounded return is closer to 10% than 12% — and that's before fees, before taxes, and before the fact that almost nobody holds a 100%-stock portfolio through retirement. Every dollar in bonds or cash lowers the expected return further.

## Problem 2: The failure rate is not small

This isn't a theoretical objection. Researchers have actually run Ramsey's numbers through history:

- Morningstar's 2026 State of Retirement Income report puts the highest safe starting withdrawal rate at **3.9%** for a 30-year retirement with consistent inflation-adjusted spending. Even retirees willing to adjust spending with the market could only start as high as 5.7% — still well short of 8%.
- Morningstar found that portfolios withdrawing at 8% had a failure rate **above 50%** over 30 years. That's not a rounding error; it's a coin flip on running out of money.
- An analysis by Early Retirement Now put the chance of running out of money at an 8% withdrawal rate at **greater than 56%**.

Nobody in mainstream retirement research endorses 8%, let alone 12%. William Bengen's original 1994 study — the source of the 4% rule — tested withdrawals against every historical period and found 4% survived them all. Bengen himself has since raised his figure to 4.7%. The gap between 4.7% and 8% is not a matter of opinion; it's the difference between a plan that survived history and one that failed it more than half the time.

## What Ramsey gets right

Fairness matters here, because Ramsey isn't wrong about everything in this conversation. His core instinct — don't live on as little as possible out of fear — is genuinely good advice for a lot of retirees. Many people do underspend in retirement, dying with large balances they never enjoyed, because the 4% rule was designed for worst-case scenarios and most actual retirements turn out better than the worst case.

He's also right that flexibility changes the math. A retiree willing to cut spending after a bad market year can safely start higher than one who refuses to adjust. And his insistence on protecting the principal — his "don't eat the goose" analogy — is the right goal. The disagreement is purely about the number that achieves it.

## What to do instead

If the 8%-or-12% idea is tempting because 4% feels too small, there are honest ways to spend more without betting your retirement on an average:

- **Start near 4% and adjust.** Morningstar's 3.9% is the conservative anchor. If markets do well early in your retirement, raise spending gradually — you get the upside without the ruin risk.
- **Use a dynamic rule.** Guardrail-style strategies let you spend more in good years and trim in bad ones. Flexibility is worth more than precision.
- **Count Social Security first.** Ramsey's own examples sometimes assume Social Security covers part of the need, which means less has to come from the portfolio. Do the subtraction before choosing a withdrawal rate — your portfolio's job is only to cover the gap.
- **Delay if you can.** Retiring at 70 instead of 60 shortens the horizon your money must cover, and a 5–6% withdrawal rate can be perfectly reasonable over a shorter retirement.

## The one-line version

Twelve percent is the market's long-run average, not a yearly guarantee — and every historical test says withdrawing 8% fails more than half the time, so start near 4%, stay flexible, and let good years raise your spending instead of letting one bad year end it.
