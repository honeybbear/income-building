# Retiring Abroad Sounded Great — Until I Did the Honest Math

> Moving overseas for a cheaper, simpler retirement is a real option — but the trade-offs go far beyond the cost of rent. Here's the checklist I wish someone had handed me before I got starry-eyed over beach-town listings.

Reviewed: October 13, 2026

## The fantasy vs. the first hard question

The fantasy: a two-bedroom apartment by the sea for a fraction of a US mortgage, fresh food at the market, healthcare for a fraction of the price, and no more lawn to mow.

The first hard question isn't "can I afford it" — it's "what does my life actually consist of, and which parts don't travel?" Family nearby. A doctor who knows your history. Familiarity with the language when you're sick, tired, or dealing with paperwork. Medicare. Before anything else, make a list of what you'd be leaving, not just what you'd be gaining.

## Healthcare: the biggest planning item

Many countries have excellent, affordable healthcare — that's genuinely true. What's also true: **Medicare generally does not cover care outside the United States.** If you move abroad permanently and drop Medicare, you lose a backstop that's very expensive to replace if you ever return.

The practical options most retirees use:

- **Local private insurance.** Affordable in many countries, but read the fine print on pre-existing conditions and age caps.
- **Keep Medicare Part B anyway.** Many expats do this as insurance against needing care back in the US — a financial cost, but one that preserves your options.
- **Self-insure locally.** Common in places where a doctor visit is genuinely cheap, but a bad plan if you develop something expensive.

Run all three against your actual health, not your optimism.

## Taxes don't disappear when you leave

US citizens are taxed on worldwide income no matter where they live. You'll still file a US return every year, and you'll likely file in your new country too (with tax treaties and foreign tax credits doing most of the heavy lifting against double taxation). Some states make it difficult to shed residency — and the taxes with it. Budget for an accountant who understands both sides, at least for the first few years.

One thing that *does* usually keep working: **Social Security can generally be paid to beneficiaries living abroad** (with a short list of exceptions). You may need to report your address and, in some cases, complete periodic questionnaires to keep payments flowing.

## The costs nobody lists in the ads

"Live on $1,500 a month" articles price the best case. Price these instead:

- **Visas and residency.** Many "retirement visas" require proof of minimum monthly income and periodic renewals, each with fees and paperwork.
- **Getting there and back.** One emergency trip home per year can erase the savings on rent. If aging parents are part of your life, multiply that.
- **Currency risk.** Your income is in dollars; your life is in another currency. A 10% swing in the exchange rate is a pay cut with no appeal process.
- **Setup costs.** Deposits, furnishing, shipping what you couldn't sell, and the inevitable "we didn't know how things worked here" mistakes of year one.
- **Importing your life.** Anything you can't buy locally — certain medications, insurance products, streaming services, your US bank relationships — gets harder.

## Try before you commit

The single best advice I can offer: **rent for at least a few months in the off-season before selling anything.** Every destination has a season when it's glorious and a season when it's hot, rainy, or empty. The paperwork, the medical system, the loneliness — all of it shows up in month three, not week two.

While you're there, handle the boring logistics in person: talk to a local immigration attorney, meet with a tax adviser, visit the clinic you'd actually use, and open the bank account. These errands are ten times harder from a US time zone.

## The honest bottom line

Retiring abroad isn't a trick — it's a trade. You're trading familiarity, proximity to family, and Medicare's safety net for lower costs, adventure, and often a better daily quality of life. That's a trade plenty of people make happily.

Just make it with open eyes: budget the real costs, protect your healthcare options, and keep the door open for as long as you can. The dream survives contact with reality — but only if reality gets a seat at the table first.
