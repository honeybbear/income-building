# The RMD Mistakes That Cost Retirees Every December

> Required minimum distributions have a hard deadline — December 31 — and the rules are full of quiet traps. Here are the mistakes that actually trip people up, and how to avoid each one before the year runs out.

Reviewed: November 1, 2026

## 1. Assuming your custodian will handle it

Your IRA or 401(k) provider will usually calculate your required minimum distribution and may even offer to automate the withdrawal. But the legal responsibility is yours, not theirs. If the calculation is based on an outdated balance, misses an account, or the automatic withdrawal never got set up, the IRS still comes to you. Every December, add up your own RMDs across all accounts and confirm each one was actually taken — don't assume a system did it.

## 2. Missing the deadline entirely

For most people, the RMD deadline is December 31 of each year. The penalty for missing it is steep: an excise tax of 25% of the amount you should have withdrawn, reduced to 10% if you correct the mistake promptly. That is not a rounding error — on a $20,000 RMD, a missed withdrawal can cost $5,000. Put the deadline on your calendar in early December, not late December, so a holiday-week snag doesn't turn into a missed year.

## 3. Getting tripped up by the first-year exception

Your first RMD year is the one exception to the December 31 rule: you may delay your first withdrawal until April 1 of the following year. It sounds generous, but it usually backfires — delaying means taking two distributions in one calendar year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket and raise Medicare premiums. For most people, taking the first RMD in its actual year is the cleaner move. Run both scenarios before choosing the delay.

## 4. Taking the wrong amount from the wrong account

RMDs are calculated from each account's balance on December 31 of the prior year, divided by a life-expectancy factor from IRS tables. Two aggregation traps catch people:

- **IRAs can be combined.** If you have three traditional IRAs, you can total their RMDs and take the full amount from just one of them.
- **401(k)s cannot.** Each 401(k) requires its own separate withdrawal. You can't cover a 401(k) RMD from your IRA, or vice versa.

Mixing these up is one of the most common errors, and it counts as a missed RMD for the account you shorted.

## 5. Forgetting the still-working exception — or assuming it applies

If you're still working at 73 or older and participate in your current employer's 401(k), you generally don't have to take RMDs from that plan until you retire. But this exception does not apply to IRAs, to old 401(k)s from previous employers, or if you own more than 5% of the company. People who changed jobs late in their career often assume all their accounts are covered. They're not — only the current employer's plan qualifies.

## 6. Giving to charity the taxable way

If you're 70½ or older and charitably inclined, a qualified charitable distribution (QCD) lets you send money directly from your IRA to a qualified charity. The amount counts toward your RMD but is never taxed as income to you. Writing a personal check to the same charity after taking the RMD as income is the same generosity with a worse tax result. One catch: the money must go directly from the IRA to the charity — once it touches your bank account, the QCD treatment is gone. And the annual QCD limit is indexed for inflation, so check the current year's figure before you give.

## 7. Ignoring what an inherited IRA now requires

The rules for inherited retirement accounts changed sharply in recent years. Most non-spouse beneficiaries now must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years, and if the original owner had already started RMDs, annual withdrawals are generally required during those 10 years too — not just a lump sum at the end. Spouses, minor children, and a few other categories are exempt from the 10-year rule. If you inherited an account, don't assume you can wait until year 10; check whether annual RMDs apply to you.

## 8. Forgetting that Roth IRAs play by different rules

Roth IRA owners face no lifetime RMDs — the account can keep growing untouched for as long as you live. But Roth 401(k)s used to require RMDs; that requirement was eliminated starting in 2024, so Roth 401(k) money now matches Roth IRAs on this point. And inherited Roth IRAs still follow the 10-year emptying rule, even though withdrawals are generally tax-free. If you're deciding between Roth conversions and traditional withdrawals, the RMD difference is a real part of the math.

## The December checklist

- List every traditional IRA, 401(k), 403(b), and inherited account you hold.
- Confirm the RMD for each one, and confirm which accounts can be aggregated.
- Verify each withdrawal actually landed before December 31.
- If you're charitably inclined and 70½ or older, consider a QCD before taking the rest as income.
- If you missed one, take the withdrawal as soon as you notice and file for penalty relief — the IRS routinely waives the penalty for reasonable errors that are promptly corrected.

RMDs are not complicated because the math is hard. They're complicated because the exceptions are easy to misremember and the deadline is unforgiving. A 30-minute review in November beats a penalty letter in April.
