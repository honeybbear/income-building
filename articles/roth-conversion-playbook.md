# Roth Conversions Before Year-End: The Playbook for Getting It Right

> A Roth conversion can be one of the smartest tax moves you ever make — or an expensive mistake you can't undo. With the December 31 deadline approaching, here's how to think about it clearly, avoid the traps that trip up even experienced savers, and decide whether this is your year to convert.

Reviewed: November 23, 2026

## What a Roth conversion actually is

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA (or an old 401(k) you roll into an IRA first) into a Roth IRA. The trade is simple: you pay ordinary income tax on every dollar you convert this year, and in exchange, that money grows tax-free and comes out tax-free in retirement — no required withdrawals, ever, for as long as you live.

That sounds like a free lunch, and in the right year it nearly is. In the wrong year, it can cost you thousands more in tax than the conversion was worth. The difference is almost entirely about timing.

## The deadline is real — and it's December 31

Unlike IRA contributions, which you can make until Tax Day, a conversion only counts for the current tax year if it's completed by December 31. A conversion you start on December 30 but that settles in January counts for next year. If you're planning to convert in 2026, don't leave it to the last week — custodians get slow in December, and a delayed transfer is a missed year.

## The three questions that decide whether to convert

**1. Will your tax rate be lower this year than in retirement?**

This is the whole game. Converting makes sense when you're in a lower bracket now than you expect to be later — a gap year between jobs, early retirement before Social Security and required withdrawals start, or a year with unusually large deductions. If you're in your peak earning years and expect a lower rate in retirement, converting now usually means paying tax at the worst possible time.

**2. Can you pay the tax from outside the IRA?**

If you convert $20,000 and owe tax on it, that tax bill should come from your savings or checking account — not from the IRA itself. Paying the tax out of the converted money shrinks the amount that gets to grow tax-free, and if you're under 59½, the part withheld for taxes can also trigger the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. If you don't have cash outside the IRA to cover the tax, that's a strong signal to wait.

**3. Will the conversion push you into a higher bracket or trigger something else?**

Converted dollars stack on top of your other income. A conversion that looks cheap can quietly push you into a higher tax bracket, raise your Medicare premiums through IRMAA surcharges, make more of your Social Security taxable, or push capital gains into a higher rate. The smart approach is to "fill the bracket" — convert only up to the top of your current bracket, and stop there. Run the numbers before you convert, not after.

## The traps that catch even careful people

**You can't undo it.** Since the 2017 tax law change, Roth conversions are permanent. There is no recharacterization, no do-over. If the market drops the week after you convert, or your tax situation changes, you're stuck with the tax bill. Convert an amount you'd be comfortable with even if things go sideways.

**The pro-rata rule.** If you have both pre-tax and after-tax (nondeductible) money sitting in traditional IRAs, the IRS doesn't let you convert only the after-tax part. Every conversion is treated as a proportional mix of the two. This is the rule that wrecks the popular "backdoor Roth" strategy for people who already hold a traditional IRA balance — check your IRA balances before you try it.

**Your required withdrawal comes first.** If you're old enough to owe a required minimum distribution, you must take that year's RMD before you convert anything. An RMD itself can't be converted — it has to come out, be taxed, and stay out.

**The five-year clock.** Each conversion starts its own five-year clock. If you're under 59½ and withdraw converted principal within five years, the 10% early-withdrawal penalty can apply. The money isn't locked away forever, but it's not freely available the day after you convert, either.

## A sensible way to do it

- **Start small.** Convert a modest amount in a low-income year and see how the tax return looks before going bigger. You can convert again next year — there's no limit on how often.
- **Convert in a down year.** A market dip is actually a good time to convert: you pay tax on a smaller balance, and the recovery happens inside the tax-free Roth.
- **Keep a paper trail.** Your custodian will send a 1099-R for the conversion. Make sure the amount matches what you intended, and keep the records — the IRS will want to see them if questions come up later.
- **Talk to a tax pro for big conversions.** If the amount is large enough to move your bracket, an hour with a CPA or enrolled agent usually pays for itself.

## The bottom line

A Roth conversion is a bet that your tax rate today is lower than it will be later. When that bet is right — a low-income year, a gap before retirement income starts, a market dip — it's one of the best moves in personal finance. When it's wrong, it's a tax bill you can't take back. Check your bracket, pay the tax from outside the IRA, mind the pro-rata rule and the December 31 deadline, and convert only what you'd be comfortable keeping converted if nothing else goes your way.
