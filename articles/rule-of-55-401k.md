# Rule of 55: How to Tap Your 401(k) Early Without the 10% Penalty

> Leave your job at 55 or later and you can pull money from that employer's 401(k) right away — no 10% early-withdrawal penalty, no age-59½ wait. Roll that same money into an IRA first, and the penalty applies for years. Here's exactly how the exception works, the trap to avoid, and the questions to ask your plan before you touch a dollar.

Reviewed: October 17, 2026

## What the Rule of 55 actually says

The IRS lets you skip the 10% early-withdrawal penalty if your employment ends during or after the calendar year in which you turn 55. "Employment ends" covers being laid off, fired, quitting, or retiring — the reason doesn't matter. Two important details:

- **It applies only to the 401(k) of the employer you separated from.** Money from an old employer's plan, or from an IRA, does not get the exception.
- **You still owe income tax.** The rule waives the penalty, not the tax. Every dollar you withdraw is added to your taxable income for the year.

Public safety workers — police, firefighters, EMS, air traffic controllers — get the same exception starting at age 50 after 25 years of service.

## The IRA rollover trap

Here's the move most financial advisors recommend — and the one that can cost you. The standard advice after a job loss is to roll your 401(k) into an IRA for lower fees and more investment options. Normally that's good advice. But the Rule of 55 lives and dies inside the employer plan: **once the money rolls into an IRA, the exception disappears.**

Say you lose your job at 56 with $400,000 in your 401(k) and you need $40,000 a year for living expenses. Leave the money in the plan and those withdrawals are penalty-free. Roll it into an IRA and each year's $40,000 costs you a $4,000 penalty — $16,000 to $18,000 burned over four years — until you hit 59½. The rollover is irreversible, so get the sequencing right: withdraw what you need from the plan first, and roll over the rest only when you're done.

## Check what your plan allows before you count on it

Some employer plans let you take partial withdrawals whenever you want; others only offer a full lump-sum distribution. If your plan is lump-sum-only, you can't cleanly take $40,000 a year — you'd have to cash out the whole thing, pay tax on all of it in one year, and then move the remainder into an IRA (losing the exception on whatever's left).

Before you make any decision, call your plan administrator and ask three questions:

1. Does the plan allow partial distributions after separation?
2. Are there minimum withdrawal amounts or frequency limits?
3. What are the plan's fees and investment options compared with an IRA?

If partial withdrawals are allowed, the play is usually: take what you need from the plan penalty-free now, leave the rest invested, and roll over only after you no longer need penalty-free access.

## What it does NOT cover

The Rule of 55 is narrow, so don't assume:

- **Not for IRAs.** Any 401(k) money you already rolled into an IRA before learning this is subject to the 10% penalty until 59½.
- **Not for SEP or SIMPLE IRAs.** They're IRAs — same rule applies.
- **Not a loan replacement.** You can also take 401(k) loans, but loans must be repaid — often within 60 days of separation — while Rule-of-55 withdrawals never need to be repaid.
- **Not all 403(b) and 457 plans qualify the same way.** 403(b)s generally follow the same rule; governmental 457(b) plans don't charge the 10% penalty at all, even without it.

## The alternative: SEPP / 72(t)

If you don't qualify — say you're 53 and the money is in an IRA — there's a separate IRS exception: **substantially equal periodic payments (SEPP)**, under section 72(t). It lets you take penalty-free withdrawals at any age if you commit to fixed annual payments for at least five years or until you turn 59½, whichever is longer. It works, but it's rigid: change the payments and the IRS retroactively slaps the 10% penalty plus interest on everything you took. SEPP is a good option for the truly stuck; the Rule of 55 is simpler and more flexible for those who qualify.

## A sensible playbook at 55+ after a job loss

1. **Confirm you qualify.** Separation in or after the calendar year you turn 55.
2. **Call your plan administrator** and confirm partial distributions are allowed.
3. **Keep the 401(k) intact** and draw only what you need, penalty-free.
4. **Estimate your taxes.** Withdrawals count as income — spread them across calendar years if possible to stay out of a higher bracket.
5. **Roll over the rest** into an IRA only after you no longer need penalty-free access, or at 59½.
6. **Don't file for Social Security early** just because the job ended — a smaller lifetime benefit is a far bigger cost than a short bridge from the 401(k).
