# How Much Can You Safely Spend in Retirement? A Practical Playbook

> A 401(k) giant made headlines this week with fresh guidance on retirement spending — because "how much can I take out each year?" is the hardest question in personal finance. Here's a practical framework for answering it yourself, without a crystal ball.

Reviewed: October 22, 2026

## Step 1: Start with the famous 4% rule — as a starting point, not an answer

You've probably heard that you can safely withdraw 4% of your portfolio in your first year of retirement, then adjust that amount for inflation each year after. It comes from a well-known 1994 study (and its successors) that tested this approach against decades of historical U.S. market data and found the money lasted 30 years in the vast majority of scenarios.

That's a useful anchor — a $1,000,000 portfolio × 4% = $40,000 a year — but treat it as a rough estimate, not a guarantee:

- It was built on a 50/50 stock-and-bond mix and 30-year retirements. Your portfolio and timeline may differ.
- It assumes you blindly keep spending through crashes, which almost nobody actually does — and that's a feature, not a bug (see Step 3).
- It was computed on historical U.S. returns. The future won't look exactly like the past.

Use 4% to sanity-check your savings target, then refine with the steps below.

## Step 2: Add up the income that isn't your portfolio

Your portfolio doesn't retire alone. Before deciding what it needs to provide, subtract the income you're confident about: Social Security (check your estimates at ssa.gov — delaying from 62 to 70 raises the monthly check substantially), pensions or annuities if you have them, and part-time income only if it's genuinely reliable.

Example math: if you need $70,000 a year and Social Security will pay $30,000, your portfolio only needs to produce $40,000 — the "gap." That gap is the number your withdrawal rate has to cover.

## Step 3: Consider a dynamic strategy instead of set-and-forget

The 4% rule's biggest weakness is that it never reacts to reality. In real life, retirees adjust: after a bad market year, spend a little less (skip the inflation raise, delay a big purchase); after a good year, let the surplus rebuild the cushion. Formal versions of this have names (guardrails, the "Guyton-Klinger" rules), but the principle is the same: flexibility is worth more than precision. A retiree willing to trim spending after a crash year can often start with a higher withdrawal rate than one who refuses to budge.

## Step 4: Respect the sequence-of-returns risk

This is the technical term for the scariest fact in retirement planning: **two retirees with the same average returns can have wildly different outcomes depending on *when* the bad years hit.** A market crash in your first five years of retirement is far more damaging than the same crash in year twenty, because you're selling investments at the bottom to fund spending.

Practical defenses: keep 1–2 years of spending in cash or short-term bonds so you never sell stocks at the bottom; front-load your caution (the first decade is when it pays most); and don't panic-rebalance into cash permanently — pacing withdrawals is the point, not abandoning growth.

## Step 5: Withdraw in a tax-smart order

Where you take the money from matters almost as much as how much you take:

- **Taxable brokerage accounts first** is the common rule of thumb — you pay only on the gains, and you let tax-advantaged accounts keep compounding.
- **Then tax-deferred accounts** (traditional 401(k)/IRA), which are taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal.
- **Roth accounts last**, since they grow tax-free and have no required withdrawals — leaving them untouched maximizes the tax-free benefit and gives you a lever to pull in high-tax years.

Watch the traps: large traditional-IRA withdrawals can push you into higher brackets, raise Medicare premiums (IRMAA surcharges kick in at higher incomes), and increase the tax on Social Security benefits. This is genuinely worth one session with a fiduciary advisor or tax pro — a small fee here routinely saves multiples of itself.

## Step 6: Recalculate every year

Your safe spending number is not a tattoo. Once a year, do a 20-minute checkup:

1. What's the portfolio worth now? Multiply by your chosen rate (e.g., 4%) to get this year's sustainable amount.
2. Does that cover the gap from Step 2, plus inflation since last year?
3. If the sustainable number fell sharply, can you trim spending, work a little, or delay a big expense?

If the number works, spend it with confidence. If it doesn't, you now know early — and early is when adjustments are cheap. A retiree who discovers a shortfall at 67 has options; one who discovers it at 82 has regrets.

## The one-line version

Estimate your gap after Social Security and other income, start around 4% of your portfolio as an anchor, stay flexible when markets fall, keep a cash buffer for the early years, withdraw tax-smart, and redo the math annually — that's the whole playbook.
