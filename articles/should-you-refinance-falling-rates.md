# Should You Refinance Now That Mortgage Rates Are Falling?

> Mortgage rates have started moving lower after months of sitting high. That doesn't mean everyone should refinance — it means it's time to run your own numbers. Here's the calm, mechanical way to decide.

Reviewed: November 7, 2026

## Start with the question rates can't answer

A refinance replaces your current mortgage with a new one — ideally at a lower rate, a different term, or both. The rate on the news is never the rate you get: your credit score, home equity, loan size, and loan type all move the number. So the first step isn't checking headlines. It's pulling your current loan statement and writing down three facts: your current rate, your remaining balance, and how many years are left on the loan.

Then ask the question that actually decides this: **how long will you stay in this home?** Refinancing costs money up front — closing costs are typically a few percent of the loan amount — and you only come out ahead if you keep the new loan long enough for the monthly savings to repay those costs.

## The break-even test

This is the one calculation that matters, and you can do it on a napkin:

1. **Find your monthly savings.** Ask a lender for a written Loan Estimate at the new rate, then subtract the new principal-and-interest payment from your current one. Ignore escrow for taxes and insurance — those don't change with a refinance.
2. **Find your total cost.** Add up the closing costs on that same Loan Estimate — lender fees, appraisal, title, and any points you're paying to buy the rate down.
3. **Divide cost by savings.** If closing costs are $6,000 and you save $200 a month, your break-even is 30 months. Keep the loan longer than that and you win; sell or refinance again before that and you lose.

Run this test on every offer. A refinance that "lowers your payment" but takes five years to break even is a bad deal for someone who might move in three.

## When refinancing usually makes sense

- **Your rate would drop meaningfully.** A small drop rarely covers the costs. A common rule of thumb is that the new rate should be at least 0.75 to 1 percentage point below your current rate — but the break-even test above is the real judge, not the rule of thumb.
- **You'll stay put past the break-even point.** The longer your horizon, the stronger the case.
- **Your credit or equity has improved since you bought.** A better credit score or a higher home value can unlock a better rate than the one you qualified for originally.
- **You want to drop mortgage insurance.** If your home has gained value and your loan balance is now below 80% of the home's value, refinancing can remove private mortgage insurance — sometimes worth as much as the rate drop itself.
- **You have an adjustable-rate mortgage that's about to adjust.** If your fixed period is ending, refinancing into a fixed rate trades uncertainty for a known payment.

## When to leave it alone

- **Your current rate is already below today's rates.** Doing nothing is the winning move, no matter how many refinance ads you see.
- **You're close to paying the loan off.** With only a few years left, most of your payment is principal anyway, and restarting a 30-year clock can cost you more in total interest even at a lower rate.
- **You'd stretch the term to get the payment down.** Refinancing a loan with 22 years left into a fresh 30-year loan lowers the monthly payment but adds eight years of interest. Compare lifetime cost, not just the monthly figure.
- **You're thinking about cash-out to pay off other debt.** Cash-out refinancing turns unsecured debt into debt secured by your home. It can make sense in rare cases, but a balance you could walk away from becomes a balance that can cost you your house. Treat that trade with real caution.
- **You can't document your income or your home appraises low.** A refinance is a full underwriting process — paperwork, appraisal, and a hard credit pull. If the appraisal comes in short, the deal can collapse after you've already paid for it.

## How to shop for one

Don't take the first offer, and don't compare offers from different days — rates move daily. Get Loan Estimates from at least three lenders on the same day, and compare the same loan type and term. Look past the headline rate to the total closing costs and whether points are included; a lender advertising a lower rate while charging two points may be more expensive than one with a slightly higher rate and no points.

And ignore the urgency. Lenders advertise refinances because refinances pay them fees. A rate that's good today will usually still be there tomorrow, and a rate that only works if you sign today is a sales tactic, not a market fact.

## The decision framework

1. **What would my new rate actually be?** Get a real Loan Estimate, not a website teaser.
2. **What's my break-even?** Cost divided by monthly savings. Will I keep this loan longer than that?
3. **What's the lifetime cost?** Compare total interest on the new loan against what's left on the old one — same term lengths, no sleight of hand.
4. **Does it solve a real problem?** A lower rate, dropping mortgage insurance, or locking in before an ARM adjusts are real reasons. "Rates are falling" by itself is not one.

Rates moving lower is good news for homeowners — but it's an invitation to check your numbers, not a command to refinance.
