# Social Security COLA 2027: What the October 14 Announcement Could Mean for Your Check

> On October 14, 2026, the Social Security Administration will announce the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment. Forecasters expect the biggest raise since 2023 — around 3.5%. Here's how the math works, what it likely means for your monthly check, and the one deduction that will eat part of it.

Reviewed: October 12, 2026

## The number everyone is waiting for: ~3.5%

The official 2027 COLA hasn't been announced yet — that happens **Wednesday, October 14, 2026**, the same morning the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases September inflation data. But two of the three numbers in the formula are already public, so forecasters have narrowed it to a tight range: roughly **3.4% to 3.6%**, with 3.5% the most-cited estimate (the Senior Citizens League projects 3.5%; AARP lands near 3.6%).

For context, the 2026 COLA was 2.8%, and 2025's was 2.5%. If the estimate holds, 2027's raise would be the largest since the 8.7% increase in 2023.

## How the COLA is actually calculated

There's no committee voting on this. The Social Security Act sets a formula: take the average Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners (CPI-W) for **July, August, and September**, compare it to the same three months from the last year a COLA took effect, and round the difference to the nearest tenth of a percent. If inflation falls, benefits don't drop — the COLA is simply zero.

The published pieces so far: July CPI-W was 327.104 and August was 328.481. If September prices merely hold at August's level, the math produces **3.4%**. A September reading above roughly 329.047 pushes it to **3.5%**. The October 14 announcement fills in that last data point.

## What each scenario means for your check

The increase applies to the December 2026 benefit, which most people receive in **January 2027**. SSI recipients get it first: the higher January 2027 payment arrives December 31, 2026, because January 1 is a federal holiday.

To estimate your own raise, multiply your current monthly benefit by the rate. At 3.5%:

- **$1,500 check → about $52.50 more** (~$1,553)
- **$2,000 check → about $70 more** (~$2,070)
- **$2,800 check → about $98 more** (~$2,898)
- A worker near the top of the scale, collecting around $4,152 this year, would see roughly **$145 more**.

The adjustment is automatic — no application, no paperwork. It covers retirement, disability, survivor, and SSI payments alike. You'll get a personal notice by mail in December, and the new amount usually appears in your my Social Security account before then.

## The deduction that eats part of the raise

Here's the part most headlines skip: for most beneficiaries with Medicare premiums deducted from their check, part of the raise flows straight back to Medicare. The standard Part B premium was $202.90 in 2026, and the 2027 amount won't be announced until November. Forecasters expect it to rise again, which shrinks the net gain — recent estimates put the average retiree's take-home raise closer to **$66 a month** after premiums, not $73.

Two practical implications. First, don't re-spend the raise in your head before January — the net amount is what matters. Second, if you pay premiums directly rather than having them deducted, this is a good season to double-check that your automatic payments reflect the new premium once it's announced.

## One honest caveat about the index itself

The COLA uses the CPI-W — an index based on urban wage earners — not an index of retiree spending. Retirees spend a larger share of their budget on healthcare, which tends to rise faster than general inflation. Consumer advocates have argued for years that the CPI-W understates seniors' actual cost of living. The COLA protects against the general drift of prices, but if your personal budget is healthcare-heavy, your real cost increase may be bigger than the official percentage.

## Three sensible moves before the announcement

1. **Run your own number.** Multiply your current benefit by 0.035 and by 0.034 — that two-point range is your likely window. Write it down.
2. **Treat the net raise as the real raise.** Until Medicare announces the 2027 Part B premium in November, any "I'll have $73 more" math is pre-premium math.
3. **Check your my Social Security account in December.** The agency posts your 2027 amount online, usually before the paper notice arrives. Verifying it yourself takes two minutes.

After October 14, the estimate becomes a fact, and this article will be updated to reflect the official number. In the meantime: a bigger raise is welcome, but the only money you can budget on is the money after Medicare takes its share.
