# Thanksgiving Dinner on a Budget: Feed the Table Without the Regret

> Thanksgiving dinner is one of the easiest meals to overspend on — a big guest list, a long shopping list, and a lot of last-minute trips. Here's a practical plan to host the feast, feed everyone well, and keep the grocery bill under control.

Reviewed: November 22, 2026

## Start with a number, not a shopping list

Before you buy anything, pick a total budget for the meal and write it down. A useful way to set it: decide what you can comfortably spend, then divide by the number of people eating. A $100 budget for eight people is $12.50 a head; for twelve people it's about $8.30. That per-person number is your reality check — if the menu you're imagining costs more than that, the menu changes, not the budget.

Tell the number to anyone who shops with you. A budget nobody else knows about is a budget nobody follows.

## Plan the menu around the turkey, not around the store

The turkey is the centerpiece and usually the biggest single cost, so decide on it first:

- **Buy the size you actually need.** A good rule of thumb is about one pound of turkey per person, which leaves room for leftovers without buying a bird twice the size of your oven. A smaller turkey also cooks faster and more evenly.
- **Frozen beats fresh on price.** A frozen turkey is almost always cheaper per pound than a fresh one, and the only cost is planning: move it to the fridge several days ahead so it thaws safely. A turkey needs roughly a day of fridge thawing for every four to five pounds.
- **Check what's already in your kitchen first.** Butter, flour, sugar, spices, broth, and canned goods hide in pantries for months. Inventory before you shop, and you won't buy a third bag of brown sugar.

## Keep the sides simple and homemade

Thanksgiving sides are where the bill quietly doubles. A few rules keep them in check:

- **Pick three or four sides, not eight.** Nobody remembers the seventh side dish. Mashed potatoes, one green vegetable, stuffing, and cranberry sauce cover the table. Each extra dish is another bag of ingredients and another hour of work.
- **Cook from scratch where it counts.** Mashed potatoes from real potatoes, stuffing from bread you already have, and cranberry sauce from a bag of fresh cranberries are all cheaper than the boxed or canned versions — and they taste better. Save the shortcuts for the one dish you genuinely don't enjoy making.
- **Store brands are fine.** For ingredients that disappear into a recipe — broth, canned pumpkin, flour, sugar — the store brand does the same job. Save the name brand for the one or two things where you can actually taste the difference.

## Make it a potluck, not a solo performance

If you're hosting family, you don't have to cook the whole meal yourself — and most guests would rather bring a dish than show up empty-handed. Assign specific dishes instead of asking "can you bring something?" People say yes more easily to "could you bring the green beans?" than to an open-ended request, and it keeps you from ending up with four pumpkin pies.

The same logic applies to drinks and desserts. A guest who brings wine or a pie is saving you a real line item, not just being polite.

## Shop once, early, with a list

Thanksgiving week is when grocery budgets die in small increments: one more trip for the thing you forgot, one more "while I'm here" item. The fix is boring but effective:

- **Write the full list from the menu, then shop once.** Check it against the pantry before you leave.
- **Go early in the week.** The stores are calmer, the shelves are fuller, and you're not making panicked last-minute substitutions at premium prices.
- **Skip the fancy extras.** Decorative gourds, specialty cheeses, and the elaborate appetizer spread are nice, but nobody's Thanksgiving memory is about the cheese plate. Spend on the meal, not the staging.

## Leftovers are part of the plan

A Thanksgiving meal that feeds people twice is a meal that cost half as much per serving. Before you cook, set aside containers and plan for the obvious: turkey sandwiches, turkey soup from the carcass, and mashed potato cakes the next day. If the turkey was the big expense, stretching it across three meals is the best return on that money you'll get all year.

## The bottom line

A good Thanksgiving dinner isn't about how much you spent — it's about who sat at the table. Set the number first, build the menu around it, let guests contribute, and shop once with a list. The feast will be just as good, and January-you won't be paying for it.
