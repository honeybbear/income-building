# Treasury Yields Above 5%: Should You Lock In Your Savings?

> The 10-year Treasury is yielding above 5% and high-yield savings accounts are advertising rates up to 5% this week. Here's how to think about locking in — without overthinking it or locking up money you'll need.

Reviewed: October 20, 2026

## Step 1: Understand what "5%" actually means for you

A 5% annual yield on $10,000 is about $500 a year — before taxes. That's real money, and it dwarfs what a typical big-bank savings account pays (often well under 1%). But savings interest is taxed as ordinary income, so your after-tax rate depends on your bracket — roughly 3.9% after tax at 5% pre-tax in the 22% bracket. Worth knowing, not a dealbreaker.

Also know what you're locking in: a bank CD or a Treasury bond fixes your rate for the full term. A high-yield savings account rate can change any time the bank decides. When rates are high, that difference is the whole decision.

## Step 2: Decide which money is eligible for locking up

Only money you genuinely won't need before the term ends should go into anything with a lockup. Run this check before you buy a single CD or bond:

- **Emergency fund: keep it liquid.** Three to six months of expenses stays in a high-yield savings account, not a 5-year CD. The whole point of an emergency fund is instant access.
- **Money needed in the next 1–3 years:** down payment, a car, tuition. This is the sweet spot for lock-in: too short for the stock market, too long to earn nothing.
- **Money you won't need for 5+ years:** probably belongs in investments, not fixed income — historically, stocks' long-term returns have beaten 5%, though with far more risk.

A common mistake: chasing the highest headline rate with money that has a job soon. The early-withdrawal penalty on a broken CD — often several months of interest — can wipe out the rate advantage entirely.

## Step 3: Pick the right vehicle

**High-yield savings account (HYSA):** fully liquid, FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor per bank. Top advertised rates are around 5% — excellent for the emergency fund and short-notice money. The catch: the rate floats. If the Fed cuts, these rates fall within weeks. Think of an HYSA as your flexible base, not your lock-in.

**Certificates of deposit (CDs):** the bank fixes your rate for the term — 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, 5 years. FDIC-insured like a savings account. Right now, shorter-term CDs often pay close to or above the 5% mark, while longer terms sometimes pay less (an "inverted" curve). That actually makes the decision easier: you can often get the best rate without committing for five years. Always check the early-withdrawal penalty before you buy.

**Treasury bills, notes, and bonds:** backed directly by the U.S. government, bought through TreasuryDirect or most brokerages. The big tax perk: Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax — meaningful in a high-tax state. You don't have to buy a 10-year bond to benefit: short-term Treasury bills (4 to 52 weeks) let you lock a rate for months instead of years.

**I Bonds:** inflation-protected savings bonds whose rate resets every six months. A complement when inflation is the worry, not the main event — the $10,000-per-person annual limit and one-year minimum holding period constrain them.

## Step 4: Consider a ladder instead of a single bet

Nobody knows where rates go from here — that's the honest truth, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing. A CD or Treasury ladder removes the need to guess: split your lock-in money across multiple terms. For example, divide $12,000 into three CDs maturing in 6, 12, and 18 months. As each matures, you either spend it (if the need arrives) or roll it into a new term at whatever rates exist then.

Laddering gives you three things a single big bet doesn't: regular access to portions of the money, partial protection if rates keep rising (you roll at higher rates), and partial protection if they fall (longer rungs still pay today's rate).

## Step 5: Watch the fine print

- **Teaser rates and requirements.** Some "5%" accounts require a minimum balance, a direct deposit, or a debit-card transaction quota. If you can't meet the conditions, you don't get the rate. Read the account terms before moving money.
- **FDIC limits.** Insurance covers $250,000 per depositor per bank. Above that, spread across banks. This matters more than people think at high balances.
- **Callable and brokered CDs.** CDs bought through a brokerage can be "callable" — the issuer can redeem them early if rates fall, which is exactly when you'd want to keep them. Bank-issued CDs generally aren't. Know which kind you're buying.
- **The tax bill.** Bank interest arrives as a 1099-INT. Budget for the tax — don't be surprised.
- **Don't over-optimize.** The difference between a 4.9% and a 5.0% account on $10,000 is about $10 a year. Pick a solid, reputable institution with a competitive rate and move on. Chasing the absolute top rate across five banks costs more in hassle than it earns.

## The one-line version

Keep the emergency fund liquid in a high-yield savings account, lock up only money you won't need with CDs or Treasuries, ladder your terms so you never have to guess where rates go next, and don't sweat the last tenth of a percent.
