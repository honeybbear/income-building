# Veterans Benefits You're Probably Overlooking: Disability, Pension, and the GI Bill

> Veterans Day just passed, and the headlines keep saying the same thing: veterans are leaving benefits on the table. The biggest reason isn't laziness — it's that nobody tells you the money is tax-free, or that a small disability rating still counts. Here's what to check, in plain English.

Reviewed: November 12, 2026

## 1. VA disability compensation is tax-free — and that changes the math

This is the benefit most veterans underestimate. Monthly VA disability compensation is completely excluded from federal taxable income under 26 U.S.C. § 104(a)(4). You don't report it on your tax return, and the VA doesn't send you a 1099 for it.

The tax-free part matters more than the headline number. A veteran rated 100% disabled with no dependents receives about $3,938 a month from the VA in 2026 — roughly $47,000 a year — and none of it counts as taxable income. To take home the same amount from a job, you'd need to earn considerably more before taxes. Even a 10% rating, the lowest level that pays, puts real tax-free money in your pocket every month.

The key point: you don't need to be "fully disabled" to qualify. If an injury or illness from your service still affects you — hearing loss, a bad knee, back pain, sleep apnea, PTSD — file a claim. Ratings are percentages, and they stack.

## 2. The VA pension: the benefit for older wartime veterans

Disability compensation gets the attention, but the VA pension is the one many older veterans miss entirely. It's a needs-based, tax-free monthly payment for wartime veterans with limited income who are age 65 or older, or permanently and totally disabled.

You don't need a service-connected injury to qualify for the pension — that's what makes it different from disability compensation. Wartime service plus limited income plus age or disability is the test. If you're a veteran over 65 living on Social Security and a small nest egg, check whether you qualify. Many veterans assume the pension is only for combat-wounded veterans. It isn't.

## 3. The GI Bill: education money that doesn't count as income

Post-9/11 GI Bill benefits pay tuition directly to the school, plus a monthly housing allowance and a book stipend — and all of it is tax-free. It covers college degrees, trade school, apprenticeships, and licensing and certification programs, not just four-year universities.

Two details people miss: the benefit can be transferred to a spouse or child in many cases, and dependents and survivors have their own program (Chapter 35, Survivors' and Dependents' Educational Assistance). If you already used your GI Bill, check whether a family member can still use transferred months. If you're a veteran's spouse or child, check whether you qualify for Chapter 35 on your own.

## 4. The VA home loan: no down payment, no PMI

A VA-backed home loan lets eligible veterans buy a home with no down payment and no private mortgage insurance (PMI) — the monthly fee conventional borrowers pay when they put less than 20% down. On a typical home purchase, skipping the down payment and PMI can save thousands a year, every year you hold the loan.

You can use the benefit more than once, and it applies to refinancing too. If you bought with a conventional loan because nobody explained the VA option, a VA refinance (IRRRL) may still cut your costs. Check your Certificate of Eligibility on VA.gov — it's free, and it takes minutes.

## 5. The tax trap: retirement pay is taxable, disability pay is not

Here's where veterans lose money every April. Military retirement pay based on years of service is taxable as ordinary income — the IRS treats it like a pension. VA disability compensation is not. When a retiree qualifies for both, federal law generally requires waiving a dollar of taxable retirement pay for each dollar of tax-free disability pay received.

That sounds like a wash, but it isn't: the same dollars arrive tax-free instead of taxable, which lowers your bill. One exception to know: Combat-Related Special Compensation (CRSC) is entirely tax-free and restores some retirement pay on top of disability compensation. If your injuries were combat-related, ask about CRSC specifically — it's a separate application, and it's the one retirees most often never hear about.

## 6. How to check what you're owed — for free

Start at VA.gov, where you can check eligibility, file a disability claim, and request your Certificate of Eligibility. For help with the paperwork, use an accredited Veterans Service Organization (VSO) — groups like the American Legion, VFW, and DAV have accredited representatives who help file claims for free.

That "for free" part is the warning: never pay a company to file your initial claim. There are businesses that charge hundreds or thousands of dollars to do what a VSO does at no cost, and some take a cut of your back pay. The VA itself warns veterans about these "claim sharks." If someone wants a fee or a percentage before you've received a dollar, walk away.

## The bottom line

If you served, run this checklist this week: file or review your disability claim, check VA pension eligibility if you're 65 or older, confirm your GI Bill status (and your family's), and pull your VA home loan Certificate of Eligibility. The benefits exist because you earned them — and unlike most money in your life, the biggest ones arrive tax-free.
