# The October-to-December Money Checklist: 9 Moves to Make Before the Year Ends

> Tax rules run on a calendar, not your convenience. With about ten weeks left in the year, these are the moves worth checking off now — while there's still time to act.

Reviewed: October 14, 2026

## 1. Check your 401(k) contributions

If you contribute to a 401(k) at work, the IRS lets you put in money until December 31 — no extensions. Pull up your year-to-date contributions and compare them against the annual limit and, if you're 50 or older, the catch-up allowance. If you're on track to fall short of what you planned, you can usually bump up your per-paycheck percentage for the remaining pay periods. Most payroll systems take one to two cycles to process a change, so mid-October is genuinely the last comfortable moment to adjust.

## 2. Spend down your FSA — or check what your plan lets you do

Flexible Spending Account money is mostly "use it or lose it." Some employers offer a grace period into early next year, and others allow a small rollover amount — but many plans still forfeit unspent balances. Log into your FSA portal, see your remaining balance, and plan for real expenses: prescription refills, a dentist visit, new glasses, sunscreen, first-aid supplies. Don't invent spending just to zero it out; but don't donate your own money back to your employer by forgetting.

## 3. Consider tax-loss harvesting in taxable accounts

If you own investments outside retirement accounts that are currently underwater, selling some of them can generate a capital loss that offsets gains elsewhere (or up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year, with the rest carrying forward). Two rules matter: the wash-sale rule means you can't buy back the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale — including in your IRA — and you should never sell just for the tax break if the investment still belongs in your plan. This is a rebalancing move with a tax bonus, not the other way around.

## 4. Think about a Roth conversion — carefully

If your income is temporarily low this year (job change, sabbatical, a gap between gigs), converting some of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA may cost less in taxes than it would in a normal year. But conversions are irreversible and the converted amount counts as taxable income now, so run the numbers on what the extra income does to your tax bracket — and to things tied to your income, like ACA premium credits or Medicare premiums down the road. When in doubt, convert a modest slice rather than going all-in.

## 5. Give to charity the tax-smart way

If you itemize deductions, contributions to qualified charities reduce your taxable income — but only if they're made by December 31, and only to eligible organizations (check the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search). Two smarter-than-cash options: donating appreciated stock lets you deduct the fair market value while never paying capital gains tax on the appreciation, and if you're 70½ or older, a qualified charitable distribution directly from your IRA counts toward required minimum distributions without being taxed as income.

## 6. Top off your HSA if you can

Health Savings Account contributions reduce your taxable income dollar for dollar, and unlike the FSA there's no use-it-or-lose-it rule — the balance is yours forever and grows tax-free. If you're in an HSA-eligible high-deductible plan and haven't hit the annual limit, extra contributions before year-end lower this year's tax bill and build a medical fund for later. Just watch the limit: exceeding it triggers penalties.

## 7. Confirm your required minimum distributions

If you're 73 or older (the current RMD starting age) and hold traditional IRAs or 401(k)s, you generally must take your required minimum distribution by December 31. Miss it and the penalty is steep. Most custodians will calculate the amount for you and can automate the withdrawal — but it's your responsibility, not theirs, to make sure it happens.

## 8. Review withholding before a surprise, not after

Did you get a raise, a bonus, freelance income, or a new spouse this year? Each of those can quietly change what you owe. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator is free and takes a few minutes. Adjusting your W-4 now gives you a couple of paychecks to absorb the change; discovering a shortfall in April costs you interest and possibly penalties. Small estimated-tax payments in the fourth quarter can also cover side-income gaps.

## 9. Harvest your gains strategically, too

Tax-loss harvesting gets the headlines, but if you're in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket (which covers a surprising amount of middle-income retirees and part-year workers), selling winners at no tax cost and rebuying can "step up" your cost basis for free. Again: only with money that already belongs in your plan, and only after confirming your actual bracket with real numbers.

## The honest bottom line

None of these moves makes anyone rich. What they do is stop the calendar from quietly costing you money — forfeited FSA balances, missed contribution room, penalties, or a tax bill you could have softened. Block an hour this weekend, work the list top to bottom, and start January with nothing hanging over you. That's what a good year-end looks like.
